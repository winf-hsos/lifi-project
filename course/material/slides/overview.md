# Lecture notes: overview (Overview)

These notes explain the deck giving an overview of the module and the project, for reading afterwards. On the first day it begins with a few quick polls on your phone. They follow the order of the slides (the numbers are frames, and build-up steps count separately) and can be read as a text of their own. The deck is shown on the first day and brought up again and again during the semester. The page that goes with it is "Overview" on the website. On the first day it is followed by a second deck, "first light", which sets up your device.

## Before we start: quick polls (Frames 3 to 22)

On the first day, before anything else, a few questions answered on your phone: scan the QR code on the slide, or open lifi.uber.space/q and type the six-digit code shown under the QR code. It is anonymous: no name, no login. Keep the page open; the next questions appear there by themselves, and the screen shows the results as soon as a question closes. The answers stay saved, and during the semester we come back to them.

**Who is here? (Frames 3 to 10).** Four questions about you: have you ever written a program, how often do you use AI chats like ChatGPT, have you ever worked with an AI agent like Claude Code, Codex or OpenCode, and how well do you think you know what happens inside a computer. Whatever the answers are, this module assumes nothing.

**A quick check (Frames 11 to 22).** Four questions that test that last self-assessment a little. How many different values can one byte hold? 256: a byte is eight bits, each bit has two states, and two to the power of eight is 256. A photo on your phone: roughly how many numbers are stored for it? Tens of millions: twelve million pixels, three numbers each for red, green and blue. At the lowest level, what does a computer work with? Numbers, in the end zeros and ones; words, pictures and sounds are built on top. You switch off your computer: what happens to what is in its RAM? It is lost, because the working memory needs power to hold anything. Then we compare the check with your self-assessment. That is not a reproach: this gap is exactly what the module closes, and at the end of the semester we ask again. Each of these questions comes back in its own session: number systems, digitization and compression, analog and digital, memory and storage.

## Part 1: why this module (Frames 23 to 43)

**Quick poll: AI in your career (Frames 24 and 25).** How important will it be for your career to understand AI and to work well with it? The answer is the starting point of this part, and one of the reasons this module exists.

**So why learn anything at all? (Frames 26 to 28).** An AI writes code for you in seconds. It explains any topic you ask about and solves most exercises you give it. So why learn anything at all? The question is fair, and it deserves a serious answer. The answer begins with a question people asked fifty years ago.

**The same question, fifty years ago (Frame 29).** When the pocket calculator arrived, people asked the same thing. Every one of you owns a calculator today, at the latest in your phone. And still nobody ever said: then let us drop maths at school. Why not? The calculator computes faster and with fewer mistakes than you do. The answer to this question is the answer to why this module exists.

**Why maths, if you have a calculator? (Frames 30 to 32).** There are three things the calculator cannot do, and that is what you learn maths for. First, writing the problem down so that the calculator can solve it: it computes what you type, not what you mean. Second, noticing that a result cannot be right: whoever has a sense of the order of magnitude sees immediately that 4,000 is wrong when 40 should come out; whoever has none writes down 4,000. Third, knowing what it cannot calculate at all.

**A computer is just a bigger calculator (Frame 33).** At heart, a computer turns numbers into numbers, billions of times a second. A photo is a list of numbers to it, a text too, a song too. And the same three questions apply: how do I write the problem down so that it can solve it, how do I notice that the result is wrong, and what can it not do at all? To judge what comes out, you have to understand what it does. Not every circuit, but the principle. That is this module.

**Pilots still learn how a plane flies (Frame 34).** Modern aircraft fly most of the time on autopilot, and pilots still learn how a plane flies. Research calls this the automation paradox: the better the automation, the more rarely a human intervenes, and the more those rare moments depend on understanding. Then the pilot has to grasp quickly what the machine is doing and why. With computers and with AI it is the same.

**The most powerful calculator humans built so far (Frames 35 to 38).** And now you have the most powerful calculator humans have built so far: an assistant that writes code for you in seconds. In this module you program with it from the first line on, and that is intended. But look at the picture: the robot types busily, and it wears a blindfold. Its answers always sound right, even when they are wrong, and it cannot check them against the real world: it does not see what its code does out there. The three things from the calculator stay with you: cutting the problem so that it can solve the pieces, checking the result, measuring. That is what you learn here, every week.

**And to understand AI (Frames 39 to 42).** The second thing this module does: it is the first step towards understanding AI, not just using it. The chain is short. AI is software, a program like any other. Software runs on a computer. And a computer turns numbers into numbers, billions of times a second. So whoever wants to understand AI first has to understand what a computer does and what software is. That is exactly where we start, at the bottom.

**What comes next (Frame 43).** This module, Digitization and Programming, is about the computer and its software: the foundation. The follow-up module, Problem Solving with AI, goes into AI itself: how it works and how to solve real problems with it. This module is its prerequisite.

## Part 2: what you will learn (Frames 44 to 52)

**Quick poll: understanding computers (Frames 45 and 46).** Would you like to have a better understanding of computers? Think of the quick check at the start. The map shows how we get there.

**The map (Frames 47 to 51).** The learning goals of the module on one map, built up in five steps. At the top the big question: how can we solve complex problems with computers? Below it four smaller questions, because a computer does four things with information: it represents it, stores it, transfers it and processes it. Under each question stands what the computer does with bits along the way, as a coloured box: making bits (orange), storing bits (green), transferring bits (magenta), processing bits (blue). Below that the concepts, eighteen of them, each belonging to exactly one question and carrying its colour; the colour stays the same for the whole semester. Last of all, right at the top and in grey, the ways of working you need everywhere: cutting problems, the model of input, processing and output, algorithms and programs, measuring and experimenting, abstraction and layers. And, all semester long, working with AI. On the first day nobody has to understand any of these concepts. You are only meant to see that they exist and where they belong.

**The map on the website (Frame 52).** Every concept has a page: a summary with guiding questions, the explanation, the slides from the session, practice questions in the exam format, further reading. The page "Overview" is the way in.

## Part 3: the project (Frames 53 to 66)

**Why a project, and not just lectures? (Frame 54).** The learning goals are set. One could simply present them, for fourteen weeks. We do something else: you build something. Why?

**Why a project? (Frames 55 to 57).** Three reasons. First, learn by doing: nobody learns to swim from a book, and concepts stick when you have built them, broken them and fixed them yourself. Second, problems first: every problem meets you on your own device before it gets a name in class. That is intended; whoever first struggles with a problem on their own understands the explanation afterwards better than someone who gets it right away. Learning research calls this productive failure. Third, reality answers: a real device does not care how convincing an answer sounds, whether it comes from you, from your neighbour or from your assistant. It works, or it does not. This is where you practise checking.

**The goal of the LiFi project (Frame 58).** There is a photo on one laptop. How does it get to the other one if the only things you are allowed to use are a small lamp and a light sensor? No cable, no Wi-Fi, no stick. That is the goal of the LiFi project: at the end, this photo crosses a gap of a hand's width, from one computer to the other. It is the goal of the project, not of the module. Nobody needs to send photos with light. The project is the way in which you experience the content of the module with your own hands.

**The device (Frame 59).** On the left the real device: a small box with two boards on its front. On the right the same thing seen from above, twice. The lamp is an RGB LED, three little lamps in one, and you tell each of them how brightly to shine. The eye is a colour sensor that reports four numbers: red, green, blue and light overall. Both sit on the outside of the device's front wall. Two such devices face each other on the table. Because the one opposite is turned around, its eye looks at my lamp and mine at its lamp. The light travels across as small coloured packets, in both directions; the way back is only used from Challenge 4 on.

**Why a lamp (Frame 60).** Everything interesting in a computer is invisible. It happens in chips, in millionths of a second, behind software that somebody else wrote. This project makes a small piece of it visible: it happens a few times a second, on your desk, where you can hold your hand in between and watch what goes wrong. That is what the project is for: so that you can touch and explore the content of the module instead of only hearing about it.

**The journey of one photo (Frames 61 to 66).** This is how the project connects to the learning goals: follow the photo on its way. It sits as a file on your laptop: memory and storage. It becomes bytes: number systems and code systems. The bytes are packed into a frame with a checksum, perhaps compressed or encrypted first: protocols, errors and redundancy, compression, encryption. Bytes become colours: symbols and information, analog and digital. The colours travel across the gap as light: signal and noise, sampling and synchronization, throughput and limits. And on the other side the photo arrives, is checked and becomes a photo again: logic and arithmetic. Every stop on the way is a concept of this module, and usually you meet it first as a problem, before it gets a name.

## Part 4: experiment: you are the receiver (Frames 67 to 80)

Before you build a receiver, you are one. For twenty minutes there is no computer and no device: the projector is the lamp, you are the sensor, and you write down every symbol you see, using the alphabet at the bottom of the slide (Frame 68). Five rounds. The first is easy; from the second on, each one takes away something you took for granted, and each belongs to one challenge.

**Round 1: four colours, slowly (Frames 69 and 70).** A countdown, then eight colours, one and a half seconds each, with a short black pause in between. Almost everybody gets all eight. Remember the feeling: this is easy.

**Round 2: eight blues (Frames 71 and 72).** Slowly, with a countdown, but eight shades of blue instead of four clear colours; you write down the number from the legend. And still wrong: not because you were too slow, but because nobody can reliably tell eight blues apart. How many states can be told apart is a question you can only answer by measuring. That is Challenge 1.

**Round 3: faster (Frames 73 and 74).** The four clear colours again, twelve of them, just under half a second each, and no black in between. At one point the same colour comes twice in a row; in the answer it is marked in yellow. Did you write it once or twice? Without a pause between them, only the time tells you that there were two. That is the problem of Challenge 2.

**Round 4: where is the message? (Frames 75 and 76).** No countdown. First the lamp shows colours that are not part of the alphabet: white, turquoise, magenta, orange. Then, without warning, a message of six colours from the alphabet, then foreign colours again. What was the message, and how did you notice where it started? Only because you knew the colours of the alphabet and not the others. In the answer the foreign colours are dots. A receiver needs exactly something like this: an agreement by which it recognises the beginning. That is Challenge 3.

**Round 5: did everything arrive? (Frames 77 and 78).** Slowly, with a countdown, eight colours. One of them flashes for only the blink of an eye, and most of you do not see it; in the answer it is marked in yellow. The unpleasant part: whoever wrote down seven did not know that something was missing. A receiver has to be able to notice whether everything arrived. That is Challenge 4.

**What went wrong? (Frames 79 and 80).** One complaint per round, in the order of the rounds: too many colours to tell apart; too fast, was that one or two; where does the message start; did everything arrive. Then the challenges appear next to them. In twenty minutes you have written the task list of the semester, as a complaint. Your device has exactly the same problems, it just does not complain. One round of the original experiment is missing on the first day, the one with the light in the room: your eye keeps correcting for a change in lighting, the sensor does not. It comes back in session 3, with the sensor.

## Part 5: 1 + 4 challenges (Frames 81 to 83)

**One new difficulty at a time (Frame 82).** One challenge to get started, then four. The Spark (can you bring the device to life?), The Alphabet (how many signals can your receiver tell apart?), The Word (can you send a whole word, and how fast?), The Listener (does the receiver find the start of a message on its own?), The Packet (can you send a real file, correct and fast?). They are the complaints from the experiment, put in order. Challenge 0 is not a competition; it ends when the LED lights up because you told it to, and the sensor opposite notices. From Challenge 1 on, all teams compete under the same conditions, and each challenge builds on the result of the one before. Because only one thing is ever new, when something breaks you usually know where to look.

**What every challenge produces (Frame 83).** Three things, worth keeping from the first day on. Measurement logs: what did you change, what did you keep the same, what came out? A description of your solution, detailed enough that another team could rebuild it; from Challenge 3 on it becomes a complete specification of your protocol, and that one another team needs in the interop test. And the log of mistakes: places where the assistant suggested something and a measurement proved it wrong. None of this is handed in or graded; it is for your team. You keep it anyway, because every challenge builds on the one before.

## Part 6: how we work (Frames 84 to 87)

Three things, one after the other. **Two people, two devices:** each of you gets a device, so together you own both ends of the link. If you only build the sender, you have to guess what the receiver sees; you can look instead. Split the work, and swap now and then. **The lamp has the last word:** AI is allowed and welcome, but your assistant knows nothing about your device. It will tell you that eight colours make a good alphabet; think of the eight blues. Measure before, measure after, and note where it was wrong. **Try the demonstrators:** for almost every concept there is a small toy in the browser: switch a byte, break a file, add noise. They are embedded in the concept pages.

## Part 7: this semester (Frames 88 to 97)

**Fourteen Mondays (Frame 89).** Every session carries a concept as its title, and underneath it what happens in the project. 1 Kick-off: overview and setup (devices, setup, Challenge 0 starts). 2 Cutting problems, algorithms and programs (your first program). 3 Measuring and experimenting (Challenge 0 check). 4 Analog and digital, symbols and information (Challenge 1 starts). 5 Signal and noise (competition, Challenge 1). 6 Number systems and code systems (Challenge 2 starts). 7 Sampling and synchronization (Challenge 2 gets faster). 8 Throughput and limits (competition, Challenge 2). 9 Protocols (Challenge 3 starts). 10 Logic and arithmetic (interop test). 11 Memory and storage (competition, Challenge 3). 12 Errors and redundancy (standardisation session, Challenge 4 starts). 13 Compression (Challenge 4, faster and shorter). 14 Abstraction and layers (final, Challenge 4). The four competitions are marked with a trophy. The dates are in the schedule in the learning platform.

**A typical Monday (Frame 90).** Every Monday runs the same way, from 15:00 to 18:15, so that you always know when to listen and when to build. At 15:00 a look back: where are you stuck? At 15:10 an input on one concept. At 15:35 an experiment that everybody watches together. At 16:05 the first workshop, at 16:55 a break, at 17:10 the second workshop. At 18:00 your task for the week, in writing. More than half of every Monday, you build.

**Every Monday (Frame 91).** Room HD0001, Mondays from 15:00 to 18:15. Bring your laptop and your device, every time, and charge the laptop before you come. Sign the attendance list every week; it is for statistics, not for grades.

**The exam (Frames 92 to 94).** 60 minutes, multiple choice, on the concepts and not on the project, everyone on their own. The project is where you meet the concepts; it does not replace understanding them. Whoever passes every challenge and still cannot say why digital transmission tolerates noise does not pass the exam. A 5 % bonus if all five of your challenges are accepted: all or nothing; what counts is the passed acceptance test with its live change, not the documentation. Practise with the questions on every concept page; they have the same format as the exam. The ranking in the competition does not count towards your grade.

**Where to find what (Frame 95).** docs.lifi-project.de is the textbook: concepts, challenges, setup. lms.hs-osnabrueck.de is the notice board: announcements, changes, the schedule. Your course folder holds your assistant and your code. lifi.uber.space/cockpit shows your link, live. And winf-hsos.github.io/lifi-concept-demos has all the demonstrators. If you get stuck, ask your assistant first, then your partner, then the team next to you, then Nicolas.

**Quick poll: how do you feel about this module? (Frames 96 and 97).** Looking forward to it, curious, a bit worried, worried: all of these are normal in the first semester. The workshop, your assistant and the order of the challenges are there for exactly that. At the end of the semester we ask again.

## Closing (Frame 98)

By the end of term, a picture crosses that gap. And along the way, more than one light will go on: you will have learned how computers represent, store, transfer and process information, and how the most powerful calculator humans built so far works. If you then say that was a module about light, something has gone wrong. Next comes the second deck, "first light": your LED lights up today.
