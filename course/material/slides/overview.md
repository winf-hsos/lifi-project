# Lecture notes: overview (Overview)

These notes explain the deck giving an overview of the module and the project, for reading afterwards. They follow the order of the slides (the numbers are frames, and build-up steps count separately) and can be read as a text of their own. The deck is shown on the first day and brought up again and again during the semester, then with whatever is currently on the agenda highlighted. The page that goes with it is "Overview" on the website. On the first day it is followed by a second deck, "from box to light", which sets up your device.

## Part 1: the goal (Frames 3 to 15)

**A photo, two laptops (Frame 4).** There is a photo on one laptop. How does it get to the other one if the only things you are allowed to use are a small lamp and a light sensor? No cable, no Wi-Fi, no stick. That is the semester in one sentence: at the end, this photo crosses a gap of a hand's width, from one computer to the other. And right away the second sentence, because it is the more important one: this module is not about sending photos with light. Nobody needs that. The lamp is a trick.

**The device (Frame 5).** A small box with two things on its front: a lamp and an eye. The lamp is an RGB LED, three little lamps in one, and you tell each of them how brightly to shine. The eye is a colour sensor that reports four numbers: red, green, blue and light overall. A thin wall between them stops the device from seeing its own lamp. Two such boxes face each other on the table. Because the one opposite is turned around, its eye looks at my lamp and mine at its lamp; so every device can send and receive. The way back is there from the start, but it is only used in the last challenge.

**Why a lamp (Frame 6).** Everything interesting in a computer is invisible. It happens in chips, in millionths of a second, behind software that somebody else wrote. You can use a computer for a lifetime without ever seeing what it really does with a photo. The device brings a small piece of that into view: it stands in front of you and blinks. You can hold your hand in between and watch what goes wrong. What happens millions of times per second inside the computer happens here a few times per second, and you built it yourselves.

**The one question (Frame 7).** How can we solve complex problems with computers? That is the question above everything else, and you will find it again on every concept page. Every week is a piece of the answer.

**What you will learn (Frames 8 to 11).** Four things, and none of them is called light. How a computer represents, stores, sends and processes information. How a computer solves a problem, and what a program really is. How to cut a big problem into small ones you can check. And how to work with an AI assistant, and where it stops. The device is the object on which all of this can be seen, because it is slow and visible. If you say at the end that this was a module about sending data with light, you have missed the most important part.

**Why learn this, if AI writes the code? (Frames 12 to 15).** You program with an AI assistant from the first line on, and that is intended. What changes because of it: typing is no longer the hard part. What stays hard is cutting a problem so that the assistant can solve the pieces, and checking whether the result is right. Your assistant writes code in seconds, but it does not know what your sensor sees, and it cannot tell whether your link works. Cutting, checking and understanding stay with you, and that is exactly what this module is for. On the slip of paper in the photo is the simplest program in the world: a box, an arrow, a box. Drawing it is your job.

## Part 2: you are the receiver (Frames 16 to 27)

Before you build a receiver, you are one. For twenty minutes there is no computer and no device: the projector is the lamp, you are the sensor, and you write down every colour you see with one letter, r, g, b or y (Frame 17). Four rounds, and each one takes away something you took for granted.

**Round 1: four colours, slowly (Frames 18 and 19).** A countdown, then eight colours, one and a half seconds each, with a short black pause in between. Almost everybody gets all eight. Remember the feeling: this is easy.

**Round 2: faster (Frames 20 and 21).** The same four colours, twelve of them, just under half a second each, and no black in between. At one point the same colour comes twice in a row; in the answer it is marked in yellow. Did you write it once or twice? Without a pause between them, the only way to know that there were two is the time. That is the problem of Challenge 2.

**Round 3: when does it start? (Frames 22 and 23).** No countdown. The lamp flickers through random colours for a while, then a message of six colours comes, then more flicker. Everybody saw every colour, and nobody can say what the message was. In the answer the message is white and the flicker grey. A receiver needs something by which it recognises the beginning. That is Challenge 3.

**Round 4: eight blues (Frames 24 and 25).** Slowly, with a countdown, eight shades of blue instead of four clear colours; you write down the number from the legend. And still wrong: not because you were too slow, but because nobody can reliably tell eight blues apart. How many states can be told apart is a question you can only answer by measuring. That is Challenge 1.

**What went wrong? (Frames 26 and 27).** The complaints of the room go onto this slide: too many colours to tell apart; too fast, was that one or two; when does the message start; did everything arrive. Then the challenges appear next to them. In twenty minutes you have written the task list of the semester, as a complaint. Your device has exactly the same problems, it just does not complain. One round of the original experiment is missing on the first day, the one with the light in the room: your eye keeps correcting for a change in lighting, the sensor does not. It comes back in session 3, with the sensor.

## Part 3: five challenges (Frames 28 to 30)

**Only ever one new difficulty (Frame 29).** Five challenges: The Spark (can you bring the device to life?), The Alphabet (how many signals can your receiver tell apart?), The Word (can you send a whole word, and how fast?), The Listener (does the receiver find the start of a message on its own?), The Packet (can you send a real file, correct and fast?). They are the complaints from part 2, put in order. Challenge 0 is not a competition; it ends when the LED lights up because you told it to, and the sensor opposite notices. From Challenge 1 on, all teams compete under the same conditions, and each challenge builds on the result of the one before: the alphabet from 1 sends the words in 2. The timing from 2 is what the listener in 3 has to find again without help. And 4 puts everything together and opens up the way back. Because only one thing is ever new, when something breaks you usually know where to look.

**What every challenge produces (Frame 30).** Three things, worth keeping from the first day on. Measurement logs: what did you change, what did you keep the same, what came out? A description of your solution, detailed enough that another team could rebuild it; from Challenge 3 on it becomes a complete specification of your protocol, and that one another team needs in the interop test. And the log of mistakes: two or three places where the assistant suggested something and a measurement proved it wrong. None of this is handed in or graded; it is for your team. You keep it anyway, because every challenge builds on the one before, and because the log of mistakes shows where the assistant's knowledge ends and yours begins.

## Part 4: one question, four questions (Frames 31 to 45)

**The map (Frames 32 to 36).** It builds up in five steps. First the big question on its own. Then four arrows to four questions: How do computers represent information? How do they store it? How do they transfer it? How do they process it? Under each question stands what the computer does with bits along the way, as a coloured box: making bits (orange), storing bits (green), sending bits (magenta), processing bits (blue). Below that the concepts, eighteen of them, each belonging to exactly one question and carrying its colour. The colour stays the same for the whole semester: where a slide is orange, it is about representing; where it is magenta, about transferring. Last of all, right at the top and in grey, five ways of working that you need everywhere and not just for one of the four questions: cutting problems, the model of input, processing and output, algorithms and programs, measuring and experimenting, abstraction and layers. On the first day nobody has to understand any of these concepts. You are only meant to see that they exist and where they belong.

**The project on the map (Frame 37).** Why this project, of all projects? Because the device touches all four questions. The photo sits as a file on the disk: storing. Its bytes represent a picture: representing. A checksum computes over them so that the receiver knows whether everything arrived intact: processing. And the light carries them across: transferring. That is why the device is the means and not the end.

**Where each challenge sits on the map (Frames 38 to 42).** The same map, one challenge highlighted per step and the rest dimmed. Challenge 0: the ways of working Input, Processing, Output, Algorithms and Programs, Cutting Problems. Challenge 1: Analog and Digital, Symbols and Information, Signal and Noise, and measuring properly for the first time. Challenge 2: Code Systems, Number Systems, Sampling and Synchronization, the first agreement, and the beginning of the layers. Challenge 3: Protocols, in full. Challenge 4: Logic and Arithmetic, Errors and Redundancy, Memory and Storage, Compression, Throughput and Limits, and, if you like, Encryption. You can see the order in the colours: first orange and magenta, representing and transferring; green and blue, storing and processing, come all together at the end, when a whole file travels through the air for the first time.

**The four questions, one sentence each (Frame 43).** Representing: to the computer the photo is a list of numbers, and the numbers are states of something physical, a voltage, a magnetised spot, a flash of light. Storing: the photo has to sit somewhere before it is sent, and somewhere after it has arrived. Transferring: the receiver does not know when a letter begins, and the light it sees is never exactly what was sent. Processing: somebody has to turn the numbers back into a picture and check whether they arrived correctly.

**What the exam is about (Frame 44).** The concepts. The project is the material on which you explain them, but it does not replace understanding them. Whoever passes every challenge and still cannot say why digital transmission tolerates noise does not pass the exam. That belongs in the first hour, not in the last.

**The map on the website (Frame 45).** Every concept has a page: a summary with guiding questions, the explanation, the slides from the session, practice questions in the exam format, further reading. The page "Overview" is the way in. It carries the same map and says for every concept in which challenge you need it.

## Part 5: how we work (Frames 46 to 49)

**Two people, two devices, one link (Frame 47).** The project is built for two people. Each of you gets a device, so together you own both ends of the link. If you only build the sender, you have to guess what the receiver sees; you do not have to guess, you can look. The two ends have different jobs; split the work and swap now and then, so that both of you have seen both sides. You program in Python, with an AI assistant in OpenCode from the first line on, and with VS Code when you want to read the code in peace.

**The lamp has the last word (Frame 48).** Your assistant knows nothing about your device. It will tell you that eight colours make a good alphabet. Is that true? Think of the eight blues. There is one honest way to find out, and that is an experiment. Measure before, measure after, and write down what the assistant got wrong. AI is allowed and welcome: for programming, for explaining what you did not understand in the lecture hall, for looking up your components. The interesting part is where it stops. How far your link reaches and how fast it goes, it can only guess. You can measure.

**The demonstrators (Frame 49).** For almost every concept there is a small toy in the browser: switch a byte, break a file, watch a transmission with noise, encrypt a text. They are embedded in the concept pages and all stand side by side on an overview page, with the concept as their label.

## Part 6: this semester (Frames 50 to 58)

This part belongs to the winter term 2026/27; the schedule in ILIAS is the binding version, and changes are announced there.

**Fourteen Mondays (Frame 51).** Every session carries a concept as its title, and underneath it what happens in the project. 28.09. Kick-off: overview and setup, kits and setup, Challenge 0 starts. 05.10. Cutting problems, algorithms and programs: your first program. 12.10. Measuring and experimenting: Challenge 0 is checked. 19.10. Analog and digital, symbols and information: Challenge 1 starts. 26.10. excursion week, no session. 02.11. Signal and noise, with the competition of Challenge 1. 09.11. Number systems and code systems: Challenge 2 starts. 16.11. Sampling and synchronization: Challenge 2 gets faster. 23.11. Throughput and limits, with the competition of Challenge 2. 30.11. Protocols: Challenge 3 starts. 07.12. Logic and arithmetic, with the interop test. 14.12. Memory and storage, with the competition of Challenge 3. 21.12. Errors and redundancy, with the standardisation session; Challenge 4 starts. 28.12. Christmas break. 04.01. Compression: Challenge 4, faster and shorter. 11.01. Abstraction and layers, with the final of Challenge 4. The four competitions are marked with a trophy.

**A Monday (Frame 52).** Every Monday runs the same way, from 15:00 to 18:15, so that you always know when to listen and when to build. At 15:00 a look back: three or four teams say where they are stuck. At 15:10 an input on one concept. At 15:35 an experiment that everybody watches together. At 16:05 the first workshop, at 16:55 a break, at 17:10 the second workshop. At 18:00 your task for the week, in writing. More than half of every Monday, you build. The competition days run differently; you hear about it a week in advance.

**Every Monday (Frame 53).** Room HD0001, 15:00 to 18:15. Bring your laptop, charged, and your kit. The attendance list goes round every week; it is for statistics, not for grades.

**The exam (Frames 54 to 57).** One hour, multiple choice, on the concepts, everybody on their own. If all five of your challenges are accepted, you get a 5 % bonus on the exam: all or nothing. What counts for that is the passed acceptance test with its live change, not the documentation. The ranking in the competition does not count. Practise with the questions on every concept page; they have the same format as the exam. The date is set by the university and announced in ILIAS.

**Where to find what (Frame 58).** docs.lifi-project.de is the textbook: concepts, challenges, setup. ILIAS is the notice board: announcements, changes, the schedule. Your course folder holds your assistant and your code. And lifi.uber.space/cockpit shows live what your lamp sends and your sensor measures. If you get stuck, ask your assistant first, then the team next to you, then Nicolas.

## Closing (Frames 59 and 60)

Five sentences to take away: one question, four questions, representing, storing, transferring, processing. Eighteen concepts, five colours, one map that keeps coming back. Five challenges, each with exactly one new difficulty. The exam is about the concepts, and the project is the place where you meet them. And: the lamp has the last word. The next step happens right away: from box to light, and your device lights up today. By the end of the semester a picture crosses the gap. If you then say that was a module about light, I have done something wrong.
