# Lecture notes: overview (Overview)

These notes explain the deck giving an overview of the module and the project, for reading afterwards. They follow the order of the slides (the numbers are frames, and build-up steps count separately) and can be read as a text of their own. The deck is shown on the first day and brought up again and again during the semester. The page that goes with it is "Overview" on the website. On the first day it is followed by a second deck, "from box to light", which sets up your device.

## Part 1: why this module (Frames 3 to 21)

**Everyone has a calculator (Frame 4).** Every one of you owns a calculator, at the latest in your phone. And still nobody ever said: then let us drop maths at school. Why not? The calculator computes faster and with fewer mistakes than you do. The answer to this question is the answer to why this module exists.

**Why maths, if you have a calculator? (Frames 5 to 7).** There are three things the calculator cannot do, and that is what you learn maths for. First, writing the problem down so that the calculator can solve it: it computes what you type, not what you mean. Second, noticing that a result cannot be right: whoever has a sense of the order of magnitude sees immediately that 4,000 is wrong when 40 should come out; whoever has none writes down 4,000. Third, knowing what it cannot calculate at all.

**A computer is just a bigger calculator (Frame 8).** At heart, a computer turns numbers into numbers, billions of times a second. A photo is a list of numbers to it, a text too, a song too. And the same three questions apply: how do I write the problem down so that it can solve it, how do I notice that the result is wrong, and what can it not do at all? To judge what comes out, you have to understand what it does. Not every circuit, but the principle. That is this module.

**Pilots still learn how a plane flies (Frame 9).** Modern aircraft fly most of the time on autopilot, and pilots still learn how a plane flies. Research calls this the automation paradox: the better the automation, the more rarely a human intervenes, and the more those rare moments depend on understanding. Then the pilot has to grasp quickly what the machine is doing and why. With computers and with AI it is the same.

**The most powerful calculator ever built (Frames 10 to 13).** And now you have the most powerful calculator there has ever been: an assistant that writes code for you in seconds. In this module you program with it from the first line on, and that is intended. But look at the picture: the robot types busily and wears a blindfold. It cannot see the device next to it. It does not know what your sensor sees, and it cannot tell whether your link works. The three things from the calculator stay with you: cutting the problem so that it can solve the pieces, checking the result, measuring. That is what you learn here, every week, and the lamp decides who is right.

**And you will understand how it works (Frames 14 to 20).** You do not only learn to work with AI, you also learn its building blocks, because almost every concept of this module is inside a language model. Text becomes tokens, every piece of a word gets a number: code systems. Meaning becomes long lists of numbers: number systems. The model itself is arithmetic, billions of multiplications: logic and arithmetic. It predicts the most likely next token: symbols and information. Plausible is not the same as true: signal and noise. The model is a lossy compression of the web; the writer Ted Chiang called it "a blurry JPEG of the web", and hallucinations are what a JPEG does to sharp edges: compression. And its context window is a small working memory: memory and storage. By the end of term, AI is no longer magic to you.

**What comes next (Frame 21).** This module, Digitization and Programming, is about how computers represent, store, transfer and process information, and how to work with AI. The follow-up module, Problem Solving with AI, goes into AI itself: how it works inside, and how to solve real problems with it. This module is the foundation; the next one builds on it. Whoever does not know how a computer represents, stores, transfers and processes information cannot see through what a language model does.

## Part 2: what you will learn (Frames 22 to 29)

**The map (Frames 23 to 27).** The learning goals of the module on one map, built up in five steps. At the top the big question: how can we solve complex problems with computers? Below it four smaller questions, because a computer does four things with information: it represents it, stores it, transfers it and processes it. Under each question stands what the computer does with bits along the way, as a coloured box: making bits (orange), storing bits (green), transferring bits (magenta), processing bits (blue). Below that the concepts, eighteen of them, each belonging to exactly one question and carrying its colour; the colour stays the same for the whole semester. Last of all, right at the top and in grey, the ways of working you need everywhere: cutting problems, the model of input, processing and output, algorithms and programs, measuring and experimenting, abstraction and layers. And, all semester long, working with AI. On the first day nobody has to understand any of these concepts. You are only meant to see that they exist and where they belong.

**What the exam is about (Frame 28).** The concepts, not the project. The project is where you meet them; it does not replace understanding them. Whoever passes every challenge and still cannot say why digital transmission tolerates noise does not pass the exam.

**The map on the website (Frame 29).** Every concept has a page: a summary with guiding questions, the explanation, the slides from the session, practice questions in the exam format, further reading. The page "Overview" is the way in.

## Part 3: the project (Frames 30 to 34)

**The goal of the LiFi project (Frame 31).** There is a photo on one laptop. How does it get to the other one if the only things you are allowed to use are a small lamp and a light sensor? No cable, no Wi-Fi, no stick. That is the goal of the LiFi project: at the end, this photo crosses a gap of a hand's width, from one computer to the other. It is the goal of the project, not of the module. Nobody needs to send photos with light. The project is the way in which you experience the content of the module with your own hands.

**The device (Frame 32).** On the left the real device: a small box with two boards on its front. On the right the same thing seen from above, twice. The lamp is an RGB LED, three little lamps in one, and you tell each of them how brightly to shine. The eye is a colour sensor that reports four numbers: red, green, blue and light overall. Both sit on the outside of the device's front wall. Two such devices face each other on the table. Because the one opposite is turned around, its eye looks at my lamp and mine at its lamp. The light travels across as small coloured packets, in both directions; the way back is only used from Challenge 4 on.

**Why a lamp (Frame 33).** Everything interesting in a computer is invisible. It happens in chips, in millionths of a second, behind software that somebody else wrote. This project makes a small piece of it visible: it happens a few times a second, on your desk, where you can hold your hand in between and watch what goes wrong. That is what the project is for: so that you can touch and explore the content of the module instead of only hearing about it.

**The project on the map (Frame 34).** The bridge: the same map, and the project below it. Why this device, of all devices? Because it touches all four questions. The photo sits as a file on the disk: storing. Its bytes represent a picture: representing. A checksum computes over them so that the receiver knows whether everything arrived intact: processing. And the light carries them across: transferring. Every concept on the map meets you in the project, usually first as a problem, before it gets a name.

## Part 4: experiment: you are the receiver (Frames 35 to 48)

Before you build a receiver, you are one. For twenty minutes there is no computer and no device: the projector is the lamp, you are the sensor, and you write down every symbol you see, using the alphabet at the bottom of the slide (Frame 36). Five rounds. The first is easy; from the second on, each one takes away something you took for granted, and each belongs to one challenge.

**Round 1: four colours, slowly (Frames 37 and 38).** A countdown, then eight colours, one and a half seconds each, with a short black pause in between. Almost everybody gets all eight. Remember the feeling: this is easy.

**Round 2: eight blues (Frames 39 and 40).** Slowly, with a countdown, but eight shades of blue instead of four clear colours; you write down the number from the legend. And still wrong: not because you were too slow, but because nobody can reliably tell eight blues apart. How many states can be told apart is a question you can only answer by measuring. That is Challenge 1.

**Round 3: faster (Frames 41 and 42).** The four clear colours again, twelve of them, just under half a second each, and no black in between. At one point the same colour comes twice in a row; in the answer it is marked in yellow. Did you write it once or twice? Without a pause between them, only the time tells you that there were two. That is the problem of Challenge 2.

**Round 4: where is the message? (Frames 43 and 44).** No countdown. First the lamp shows colours that are not part of the alphabet: white, turquoise, magenta, orange. Then, without warning, a message of six colours from the alphabet, then foreign colours again. What was the message, and how did you notice where it started? Only because you knew the colours of the alphabet and not the others. In the answer the foreign colours are dots. A receiver needs exactly something like this: an agreement by which it recognises the beginning. That is Challenge 3.

**Round 5: did everything arrive? (Frames 45 and 46).** Slowly, with a countdown, eight colours. One of them flashes for only the blink of an eye, and most of you do not see it; in the answer it is marked in yellow. The unpleasant part: whoever wrote down seven did not know that something was missing. A receiver has to be able to notice whether everything arrived. That is Challenge 4.

**What went wrong? (Frames 47 and 48).** One complaint per round, in the order of the rounds: too many colours to tell apart; too fast, was that one or two; where does the message start; did everything arrive. Then the challenges appear next to them. In twenty minutes you have written the task list of the semester, as a complaint. Your device has exactly the same problems, it just does not complain. One round of the original experiment is missing on the first day, the one with the light in the room: your eye keeps correcting for a change in lighting, the sensor does not. It comes back in session 3, with the sensor.

## Part 5: 1 + 4 challenges (Frames 49 to 51)

**One new difficulty at a time (Frame 50).** One challenge to get started, then four. The Spark (can you bring the device to life?), The Alphabet (how many signals can your receiver tell apart?), The Word (can you send a whole word, and how fast?), The Listener (does the receiver find the start of a message on its own?), The Packet (can you send a real file, correct and fast?). They are the complaints from the experiment, put in order. Challenge 0 is not a competition; it ends when the LED lights up because you told it to, and the sensor opposite notices. From Challenge 1 on, all teams compete under the same conditions, and each challenge builds on the result of the one before. Because only one thing is ever new, when something breaks you usually know where to look.

**What every challenge produces (Frame 51).** Three things, worth keeping from the first day on. Measurement logs: what did you change, what did you keep the same, what came out? A description of your solution, detailed enough that another team could rebuild it; from Challenge 3 on it becomes a complete specification of your protocol, and that one another team needs in the interop test. And the log of mistakes: places where the assistant suggested something and a measurement proved it wrong. None of this is handed in or graded; it is for your team. You keep it anyway, because every challenge builds on the one before.

## Part 6: how we work (Frames 52 to 55)

Three things, one after the other. **Two people, two devices:** each of you gets a device, so together you own both ends of the link. If you only build the sender, you have to guess what the receiver sees; you can look instead. Split the work, and swap now and then. **The lamp has the last word:** AI is allowed and welcome, but your assistant knows nothing about your device. It will tell you that eight colours make a good alphabet; think of the eight blues. Measure before, measure after, and note where it was wrong. **Try the demonstrators:** for almost every concept there is a small toy in the browser: switch a byte, break a file, add noise. They are embedded in the concept pages.

## Part 7: this semester (Frames 56 to 63)

**Fourteen Mondays (Frame 57).** Every session carries a concept as its title, and underneath it what happens in the project. 1 Kick-off: overview and setup (kits, setup, Challenge 0 starts). 2 Cutting problems, algorithms and programs (your first program). 3 Measuring and experimenting (Challenge 0 check). 4 Analog and digital, symbols and information (Challenge 1 starts). 5 Signal and noise (competition, Challenge 1). 6 Number systems and code systems (Challenge 2 starts). 7 Sampling and synchronization (Challenge 2 gets faster). 8 Throughput and limits (competition, Challenge 2). 9 Protocols (Challenge 3 starts). 10 Logic and arithmetic (interop test). 11 Memory and storage (competition, Challenge 3). 12 Errors and redundancy (standardisation session, Challenge 4 starts). 13 Compression (Challenge 4, faster and shorter). 14 Abstraction and layers (final, Challenge 4). The four competitions are marked with a trophy. The dates are in the schedule in the learning platform.

**A typical Monday (Frame 58).** Every Monday runs the same way, from 15:00 to 18:15, so that you always know when to listen and when to build. At 15:00 a look back: where are you stuck? At 15:10 an input on one concept. At 15:35 an experiment that everybody watches together. At 16:05 the first workshop, at 16:55 a break, at 17:10 the second workshop. At 18:00 your task for the week, in writing. More than half of every Monday, you build.

**Every Monday (Frame 59).** Room HD0001, Mondays from 15:00 to 18:15. Bring your laptop and your kit, every time, and charge the laptop before you come. Sign the attendance list every week; it is for statistics, not for grades.

**The exam (Frames 60 to 62).** 60 minutes, multiple choice, on the concepts, everyone on their own. A 5 % bonus if all five of your challenges are accepted: all or nothing; what counts is the passed acceptance test with its live change, not the documentation. Practise with the questions on every concept page; they have the same format as the exam. The ranking in the competition does not count towards your grade.

**Where to find what (Frame 63).** docs.lifi-project.de is the textbook: concepts, challenges, setup. lms.hs-osnabrueck.de is the notice board: announcements, changes, the schedule. Your course folder holds your assistant and your code. lifi.uber.space/cockpit shows your link, live. And winf-hsos.github.io/lifi-concept-demos has all the demonstrators. If you get stuck, ask your assistant first, then your partner, then the team next to you, then Nicolas.

## Closing (Frame 64)

By the end of term, a picture crosses that gap. And on the way there you have learned how computers represent, store, transfer and process information, and how the most powerful calculator ever built works. If you then say that was a module about light, something has gone wrong. Next comes the second deck, "from box to light": your LED lights up today.
