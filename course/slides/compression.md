# Lecture notes: say it shorter (Compression)

Input for session 13, deck 15, the second of the session's two decks. These notes explain the slides for you to read afterwards; they follow the order of the slides but can be read as a text on their own. The slide references count frames, so every build-up step separately, exactly like the counter in the deck. In the first deck of the session ("why is it still not done?"), compression was the third of the three levers, and the cheapest. Here the question is where in a file there is any air at all and what the word for it is (Part 1), how you let it out and what sits inside every ZIP (Part 2), and where that stops: at the photo (Part 3).

## Part 1: why anything shrinks

**Nothing is gone, only the air between (Frame 4).** A baling press turns loose cardboard into the same cardboard on a fraction of the space. Nothing has disappeared, only the air in between. From this follow the two questions of this deck: where does the air sit in a file? And the less comfortable one: what do you do when there is none left?

**Compression as a box, twice (Frame 5).** Before we start, the model from session 2: input, processing, output. Compression is two such boxes. A file goes into the first, `pack()`, and a smaller one comes out. The smaller one goes into the second, `unpack()`, and what comes out must be the same file, bit for bit; otherwise it was not compression but loss. Everything else is the question of what may happen in the first box so that the second can still manage. In Part 3 we add boxes where only something that looks like the original comes out at the end.

**First kind of air: stretches come back (Frame 6).** When a text file is half the size after packing and comes out character for character the same on unpacking, obviously nothing has been left out. What happened: the usual way of writing things down gives space away. On the slide, "the sun" stands three times in the same sentence, and a file that stores the sentence the usual way spells out the seven characters three times. That is the first kind of air, contextual redundancy: a stretch follows from its surroundings. In Part 1 we only look at where it sits; how you let it out is Part 2.

**Second kind of air: letters are unevenly frequent (Frame 7).** The second kind has nothing to do with repetition. In English almost every eighth letter is an `e` (12.7 per cent), and the `z` appears 180 times less often (0.07 per cent; figures from Lewand, Cryptological Mathematics, 2000). A fixed code, as you know it from Code Systems, pays the same for both: eight bits for the `e`, eight for the `z`. That is wasted room too, and it is there even in a text in which not a single word repeats: alphabetic redundancy, the characters are unevenly frequent.

**The word for both: redundancy (Frame 8).** Both kinds of air have one name, redundancy, and each kind its own, contextual and alphabetic redundancy. Remember the idea of information from Symbols and Information: information measures surprise, and surprise is not a yes or no but a degree. A stretch that has been there before can be named instead of spelled again, and naming is cheaper; not because it is predictable, but because the name is shorter than the stretch. A frequent letter surprises less than a rare one, so its code may be shorter and the rare one's longer; that is probability, not certainty. A random character is a full surprise every time, so there is nothing to save, and that is the reason no packer makes everything smaller; we will see it again in Part 2. A fixed code pays every character as if it were a full surprise; the difference is the redundancy. Compression spends bits on surprise and none on the rest. That is why nothing is missing afterwards.

## Part 2: two tricks, and a zip

**Trick one: point back (Frames 10 to 12).** The sentence from Part 1, 49 characters. In the first step, the second and third "the sun" become a short placeholder with an arrow pointing back to the first occurrence; the line gets visibly shorter. In the second step the placeholder gets its two numbers: 19 characters back, 7 characters long, that is enough as an instruction; for the third, 18 back. 49 characters become 35 plus two pointers, and a pointer costs about as much as two characters. That, in essence, is the first thing every packer does: it keeps the last few thousand characters in mind, and when something comes round again, it writes only where it was and how long it is. Lempel and Ziv described this in 1977; the technical term is dictionary coding, and it sits inside every ZIP.

**Trick one: what it looks like in the file (Frame 13).** At the top, as in a hex editor: every character one byte, 49 bytes. Below it the packed version. In our small format a pointer needs three bytes: a marker `ff`, so the reader knows that no character is coming now, then the number of characters back, then the length. `ff 13 07` means: 19 back, 7 long. 35 characters plus two times three bytes, 41 instead of 49. On a sentence this short that is little, on a book with long repetitions it is a lot. And you can see why a pointer for two characters never pays: it costs three. A real packer spends bits instead of bytes on this and hides the marker in a flag bit; the idea is the same.

**Trick one on a picture: run length (Frame 14).** The simplest form of the idea, on a picture. Walk along the row, count how many equal points come one after another, and write one byte per run: one bit for the colour, seven for the length. For a picture with areas of one colour that is six bytes instead of eight. Not the world, but the idea carries: the larger the areas, the more it saves. You could program this yourself in an afternoon.

**Trick one: run length, the bad case (Frame 15).** The same method let loose on a chessboard. Every point differs from its neighbour, so 64 runs of length one, so 64 bytes instead of eight: eight times as big. To our eye a chessboard is a pattern, to this method it is not, because it knows only the one question, how many equal points come next. A method only ever recognises the regularity it is looking for. That is why serious packers check their result and, when in doubt, send the raw data.

**Trick two: the frequent letter gets the short code (Frame 16).** Now bring in the second kind of air, the uneven frequency, and keep two promises for it. In session 4 we said about Morse: the frequent `e` gets the shortest code, a single dot, the rare `q` four signs, and we will come back to that. Morse pays for it, though, with the pause as a third symbol, because one dot is `e` and two dots are `i`, and without a pause nobody would know whether `i` or `e e` was meant. In session 6 we said: codes may differ in length, and the receiver still reads them without separators, as long as no code is the beginning of another. What stayed open was who gets the short codes; Morse says: the frequent one. What is missing is the recipe that turns a count into the codes, and that comes now. The technical term for this second idea is entropy coding; it works even when nothing repeats at all.

**Trick two: count first (Frame 17).** A magic word, `abracadabra`, and compression feels like magic until you see the trick. Eleven letters, five different ones: `a` five times, `b` and `r` twice each, `c` and `d` once each. With a fixed code, as in session 6, every letter needs three bits, so the word needs 33. The `a` pays exactly as much as the `d`, although it appears five times and the `d` once. That is where the air is.

**Trick two: build the tree (Frames 18 to 22).** David Huffman, 1951, a student at MIT. His professor offered a choice between an exam and a term paper; the term paper was "find the shortest code", and Huffman found it shortly before he was going to give up and study for the exam after all. The rule is a single one: take the two rarest characters, join them into a node whose number is their sum, and repeat until only one node is left. Step by step: `c` and `d`, the ones, become a 2 (Frame 19). Then `b` and `r`, the twos, a 4 (Frame 20). Then 2 and 4 a 6 (Frame 21). Finally `a` and 6 the root, 11, as many letters as the word has (Frame 22). What occurs often stays close to the root; what occurs rarely drifts to the bottom.

**Trick two: read the codes off the tree (Frame 23).** The path from the root to a letter is its code: a 0 at every left branch, a 1 at every right one. The `a` hangs directly on the root and gets a single bit, `0`. The others get three: `b = 100`, `r = 101`, `c = 110`, `d = 111`. Paid out: five times one bit for the `a`, three each for the other six letters, 5 + 18 = 23 bits instead of 33, nearly a third less. And on reading, the same word comes back letter for letter: all the saving comes from the `a`, the other four still cost three bits, so nothing is lost.

**Trick two: why it reads itself (Frame 24).** The worry from session 6: codes of different lengths without separators, how is the receiver supposed to know where a character ends? The tree answers that as a side effect. Letters hang only on leaves, so no code can be the beginning of another. Read from the left and start over at every leaf, and you never fall into the trap: `0` is complete, an `a`; `100` is complete, a `b`; `101`, an `r`. The counterexample that went wrong in session 6 was `a = 0` and `b = 01`: there `01` has two readings, because the `a` would not be a leaf but a stop on the way to the `b`. In a Huffman tree that cannot happen.

**Try it yourself (Frame 25).** [The Text Squeezer](https://winf-hsos.github.io/lifi-concept-demos/text-squeezer/) packs any text as you type with the same two tricks and shows three bars: the fixed code for the text's alphabet, the state after pointing back, and the state after both tricks, table included. One letter four hundred times shrinks to a few pointers; that is the lower limit. Random letters grow, because marker and table cost more than they save: no redundancy, nothing to remove. A log file ends up under half.

**And that is a ZIP (Frame 26).** The thing you have been clicking on since school. A ZIP does exactly today's two tricks, one after the other: first point back, then short codes for the frequent. The pair is called Deflate, and PNG, the picture format, is the same pair inside, only with a picture in front. The five bars are real measurements from 13 September 2026 with Python's `zipfile` at level 9: a log file of 1200 almost identical lines shrinks to 19 per cent, because every line has almost been there before. A text to 45 per cent. The photo raw to 68 per cent; a photo has no more air than that. The photo as a PNG to 100 per cent, because it already is one. And random bytes get bigger, 101 per cent. It is not the size that decides, but the predictability. Why that has to be so, the next slide says.

**Why the random bytes could not shrink (Frame 27).** The fundamental argument, and it is one of the nicest of the semester, because it has nothing to do with technology. Suppose somebody promises a method that makes **every** file at least one bit smaller, losslessly. With three bits you can write eight files, with fewer than three bits only seven. Put eight things into seven drawers and two of them end up in the same drawer, and then on unpacking nobody knows any more which one was meant. No computer in the world helps against that. Every lossless method makes some files smaller and others necessarily bigger. The 101 per cent from before are not bad luck, they are mathematics.

## Part 3: when redundancy runs out

**How much redundancy a picture has (Frame 29).** The two extremes and the parrot in between, all three 256 by 256 points, 196 608 bytes raw. On the left a picture of a single colour: total redundancy, every point can be predicted from the first, as a PNG 569 bytes, and most of that is the file header. On the right noise, every point a full surprise: as a PNG 197 172 bytes, more than raw, because the bookkeeping comes on top. The parrot sits in between, but closer to the noise than to the single colour: 93 107 bytes, a factor of two. A photo has redundancy, neighbouring points resemble each other, and PNG uses that; but it has much less than you would think, and this factor of two is all there is to get losslessly.

**What PNG does before it packs (Frame 30).** Where does the parrot's factor of two come from? Before packing, PNG takes a step that takes the idea from Part 1 literally: it predicts every point from its neighbour and stores only the difference. The slide shows twelve real red values from one row of the parrot (row 150 from column 150): 251, 250, 249, 249, all close together. As differences from the left neighbour these become lots of small numbers around zero, −1, −1, 0, +2, and the same few keep coming round: contextual redundancy between neighbouring points, and exactly that is food for pointing back and for short codes. The same row from the noise delivers differences like −29, +48, −124, as large and as random as the values themselves; there the step gains nothing. PNG has several such prediction rules, some using the point above as well, and picks the best one for each row. The difference from JPG in one sentence: PNG stores the difference exactly, JPG rounds it away.

**The parrot, honestly (Frame 31).** The same parrot, written down three times, with the picture next to it on the left. Raw, that is 256 by 256 points times three bytes, 196 608 bytes, 44 hours at ten bits per second. As a PNG, so packed losslessly, 93 107 bytes, 21 hours; that is the factor of two from Frame 29, and a photo's redundancy gives no more than that. As a JPG at quality 75 it is 10 587 bytes, a little over two hours, and from two metres away you see no difference. The jump from 93 to 10 kilobytes does not come from a better packer but from a different kind of method: JPG leaves out detail, and that is information, not redundancy. What that means and how far you can push it, the next frames show.

**The same parrot, four times (Frame 32).** Four real files, shown at the same size, with their measured sizes: the PNG with 93 107 bytes, a JPG at quality 75 with 10 587 bytes, a JPG at quality 10 with 3 081 bytes, and a JPG with 128 by 128 points at quality 25 with 2 032 bytes. Between the first and the second picture lies a factor of nine, and from two metres away you see no difference. That is what JPG does: it leaves out detail that an eye hardly misses, fine colour differences above all, and keeps the edges in brightness. On the third you slowly begin to see it. The fourth has half as many points per side and is under two kilobytes. It is a parrot. It is not the same parrot.

**What the eye and the ear do not notice (Frame 33).** How does JPG get from 93 to 10 kilobytes when the redundancy is already used up? Not with a better packer, but with a model of the eye. The eye sees edges in brightness sharply but edges in colour blurred; in a feather or in gravel it sees the texture, not the single point; and it cannot tell 250 from 251. Everything that falls into these gaps JPG may round coarsely or leave out, and nobody notices until you overdo it; then the blocks appear. MP3 does the same with a model of the ear: a loud tone masks quiet tones that sound at the same time and lie close to it in pitch (masking); nobody hears very high frequencies; and just after a loud bang the ear is deaf for a moment, in any pitch. Both methods keep what you would perceive and round the rest away; that is called perceptual coding. That is why they get so much further than lossless, and that is why there is no way back: what has been rounded is not hidden, it is gone. How JPG calculates this in detail (blocks of 8 by 8 points as a mixture of cosine patterns, the rounding of the fine parts) is left out; the concept page has a video that demonstrates it.

**A one-way street (Frame 34).** The one difference that decides everything. With a ZIP, unpacking is the reverse of packing, bit for bit: afterwards you hold the original in your hand. With a JPG there is no reverse: what is gone is gone, and you hold something that looks like the original. The two kilobytes contain everything the receiver will ever learn about this picture; the other 91 vanished during packing, they are not hidden. Edit a photo ten times and save it as a JPG each time, and you lose ten times.

**Lossless or lossy (Frame 35).** Lossy methods are not unreliable, they reliably do something else. Must exactly this file arrive, because in the final the checksum compares? Then lossy is out. Must a presentable picture arrive? Then it is the strongest lever you have, stronger than any alphabet. The task decides, not the tool, and you settle this question **before** you spend an hour optimising.

**The receiver has to know (Frame 36).** Not a single bit wrong, and still garbled characters: the receiver did not know the method. Packed data is a notation, and whoever does not know the notation reads nonsense. Method and settings belong in your specification, right next to the preamble and the character code. The same lesson as in session 6: the same bits, two tables, two meanings.

**The workshop task (Frame 37).** First, zip three files of your own and explain the three ratios; what matters is the explanation, not the number. Second, bring the parrot under two kilobytes and write down what you gave up for it: fewer points or coarser ones, and why. Third, write method and settings into the specification; that is mandatory for Challenge 4.

**To close (Frame 38).** Two kilobytes, shown point for point, without smoothing: 128 points across the height of the screen. It is still a parrot. What is missing, you decided, and that is the difference between a packer and you.
