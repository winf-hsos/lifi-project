# Lecture notes: "who else is listening?" (Encryption)

These notes explain the deck on the concept Encryption for you to read afterwards. They follow the order of the slides (the numbers are frames, build-up steps count separately) and can be read as a text on their own. The deck is the extra material for Challenge 4: whoever is done early in the final encrypts their payload and lets another team try to read along.

## Part 1: light is public (Frames 3 to 6)

**The postcard (Frame 4).** Everyone on the way reads what is written on a postcard: the postman, the sorter, the neighbour who takes it out of the box. Nobody has to break anything open to do it. Your light is a postcard, and there is no envelope for light. The question of the deck: how do you write a postcard that everyone can read and nobody can understand?

**The third sensor (Frame 5).** Two devices, one light cone, and a third device whose sensor stands in the same cone. It reads the same values as your receiver, r=1086, g=469, b=145, and you do not notice. That is not a trick by the neighbours and not a gap in the setup, it is the nature of light: it spreads out. A tube around the LED helps against stray light, not against a sensor at the mouth of the tube. A colour alphabet of your own does not help, because whoever measures along for a while counts your colours and tries the few possible mappings. Sending faster does not help, because the sensor next door is the same as yours; it records anyway and analyses later at leisure. Everything that comes out of the light, the listener has too.

**Encryption as a box, twice (Frame 6).** The same way of thinking as with compression, the IPO model: the message goes into `encrypt()`, and out come bytes that look like noise; those go into `decrypt()`, and out comes the message. The difference from compression is the second input. `pack()` needs nothing but the file; `encrypt()` needs the file and a secret, the key, and `decrypt()` needs the same key. What comes out of the first box, anyone may see. What went in, only whoever has the key gets back. Everything else in the deck is the question of what this second input looks like: how you compute with it, how long it has to be, how both sides come to know it, and what it cannot do.

## Part 2: one gate, one key (Frames 7 to 16)

**You know the gate already (Frame 8).** XOR from the toolbox of Logic and Arithmetic: 1 when the two inputs differ. There we built a parity bit from it. The same gate encrypts, and the whole method fits in one sentence: where the key has a 1, the bit flips; where it has a 0, the bit stays.

**XOR with a key byte (Frames 9 to 11).** The byte `0110 1101`, an m, and the key byte `1010 1010`. Position by position: 0 against 1, different, so 1. Then 1 against 0, so 1. Then 1 against 1, the same, so 0. And so on. The key flips every second position, and out comes `1100 0111`, hex c7. The m has become a byte that matches no letter and looks like any old byte, and that is exactly the intention. Two things did not happen: the byte did not get longer, and nothing was thrown away. OR would have given `1110 1111`, AND `0010 1000`, and with AND bits would be lost that nobody can bring back. XOR only flips.

**Once more, and it is back (Frames 12 and 13).** The receiver does exactly the same thing, XOR with the same key: `1100 0111` against `1010 1010` gives `0110 1101`, the m. The positions the key flipped, it flips back; the others it never touched. No inverted key, no subtracting, no reverse operation, because XOR is its own reverse. Encrypting and decrypting are the same operation with the same key. That makes XOR the simplest cipher there is and, used right, one of the strongest.

**The key of all zeros (Frame 14).** A key `0000 0000` flips nothing. The program runs, the transmission works, and the neighbouring team reads along. Encryption that does nothing looks from the inside exactly like encryption that does everything. Check it the way you check everything: look at the bytes that actually go over the link.

**What it looks like in the file (Frame 15).** "meet at noon" as twelve bytes, `6d 65 65 74 20 61 74 20 6e 6f 6f 6e`. Below it the key "lifi", repeated as often as the message is long: `6c 69 66 69 6c 69 66 69 6c 69 66 69`. Below that what is sent, byte for byte XOR: `01 0c 03 1d 4c 08 12 49 02 06 09 07`. Twelve bytes in, twelve out, the same length, the same sending time, and not one byte that anyone can read. The key may be a word you can remember. Whether that is wise, Part 3 settles.

**The demonstrator (Frame 16).** The Scrambler: type in text and key, see the three rows, press "decrypt", and the text is back. The second example has a key of one character; "try all keys" leads to the next part; with a longer key the button works out how many attempts that would be and how long they would take. And the parrot: with a three-byte key it is still there.

## Part 3: how long is long enough (Frames 17 to 28)

**Anyone may know the method (Frame 18).** Your specification says that you encrypt with XOR, and all the teams read it. That does not make the encryption pointless: knowing the method is not knowing the key. This is the oldest rule of the field, written down by Auguste Kerckhoffs in 1883: security may depend on the key alone, never on the method staying secret. Methods get observed, passed on, rebuilt. Your browser encrypts with methods from the textbook and is secure all the same, because the key is different every time and nobody else has it. So everything hangs on the key, and the next question is how much a key can carry.

**One byte of key: 256 tries (Frame 19).** A team encrypts the photo with a key of one byte, a7. The first 16 bytes the neighbouring team reads: `e5 ea 91 a7 a4 a7 a7 a7 a7 a7 91 a7 a7 a7 8f a7`, which looks like noise. But one byte of key has only 256 possible values, and a program tries them all in a fraction of a second. Key 00, 01, 02: nonsense. Key a7: `42 4d 36 00 03 00`, that is "BM", the header of a bitmap, and behind it the parrot. Checked: for our picture there is exactly one key out of 256 that gives a valid header. Looking like noise is not the same as being noise. A key has to have so many possibilities that trying them all is hopeless; today's keys have 128 bits and more, more values than there are atoms within reach.

**Why longer is better (Frame 20).** Every byte of key multiplies the possibilities by 256. At a billion tries a second, the order of one graphics card: two bytes are 65 536, instant; four bytes are 4 294 967 296, four seconds; eight bytes are 18 446 744 073 709 551 616, 585 years; sixteen bytes, 128 bits, are 3.4 times 10 to the power of 38, more than the universe is old in seconds, by many orders of magnitude. That is why keys today are 128 or 256 bits long: not sixteen times harder than one byte, but out of reach. The billion a second is an order of magnitude, not a measurement; that changes nothing about the conclusion.

**Your password is a key (Frame 21).** The same arithmetic for something you use every day. Eight lowercase letters are 26 to the power of 8, about 200 billion, three and a half minutes. Eight characters from the full set of 95, upper case, lower case, digits, symbols: 95 to the power of 8, 77 days. Twelve lowercase letters: 26 to the power of 12, three years. Twelve mixed: 95 to the power of 12, 17 million years. That is why systems ask for symbols, and that is why length is the stronger lever all the same: four more characters gain more than the whole character set, because the length sits in the exponent and the character set only in the base. And the red line: attackers do not start at aaaaaaaa. They take lists, passwords leaked in earlier breaches, dictionaries, names, years, the usual substitutions of a by @ and o by 0, and try those first. A word from a list falls in under a second, however long it is: password123, summer2026, your cat's name. On haveibeenpwned.com you can check whether your own already sit in such lists.

**Three bytes, and the parrot is still there (Frame 22).** Three bytes of key, 16 million possibilities; a program tries those in a fraction of a second too. But you do not even need to try. The neighbouring team simply displays what it read as a picture: the colours are wrong, but the parrot is there all the same, outline, beak, plumage. Encrypted is not the same as unrecognisable.

**Why the pattern survives (Frame 23).** Three points of the black background are `00 00 00`, three times. XOR with `5a c3 96` gives `5a c3 96` three times: same input, same key, same output. Three points of the plumage, `e0 29 13`, `da 0d 0d`, `d9 08 0b`, give three different outputs. The background becomes one colour, the plumage stays colourful. A repeated key replaces every byte with another one, but always in the same way, and a longer key changes nothing about that as long as it repeats. That is why every serious method makes sure that equal inputs give different outputs, for instance by letting every byte also depend on the one before; that is material for later. The lesson for now: even encrypted, a picture can still give something away.

**A key as long as the picture, used once (Frame 24).** Take a random key as long as the picture, 196 608 bytes, and use it only this one time. Then every result byte is random on its own, every possible message of the same length fits what was sent equally well, and no statistics can tell them apart. That is the one-time pad, and Claude Shannon proved in 1949 that it cannot be broken, with all the computing in the world. It has a price.

**How the key gets to your partner (Frame 25).** The key has to get to your partner, secretly, and with the one-time pad it is as big as the message itself. Three ways. Over the link, in the clear: the neighbouring team reads it along, and everything after it comfortably too. Over the link, encrypted: encrypted with what? That would need a key the partner already knows, the same question one level down. That leaves the third way: hand it over where nobody is listening. In the course that is easy, you sit at the same table, a slip of paper does it. Between two computers that have never met, exactly that is the hard question of the whole field.

**Strangers: a padlock instead of a slip of paper (Frames 26 to 28).** Your browser has never met the shop and cannot give it a slip of paper; still, both have to end up with the same secret. The idea that solves this needs only one picture: a padlock that anyone can snap shut but only one key opens, and that key stays with the shop, always. First step: the shop sends the open padlock over the line; the neighbours see an open padlock and can do nothing with it. Second step: the browser rolls a fresh secret, puts it inside, snaps the lock shut and sends it back; on the way nobody can open it, not even the browser itself. Third step: the shop opens it with its key and reads the secret. Now both know it, and from here on they encrypt with it by XOR just as you do with your slip of paper. The line saw an open lock and a closed one, but never the key and never the secret. How such a lock is built out of mathematics is material for later semesters (Diffie and Hellman, 1976).

## Part 4: what a key does not do (Frames 29 to 35)

**Flipped bits go straight through (Frame 30).** On the way a disturbance flips a bit, or the neighbouring team flips it on purpose. The receiver decrypts and gets a wrong bit at that position, because XOR turns every received bit into some bit; nothing about it says "something is wrong here". In the example a bit flips in the last o of "meet at noon": 6f becomes 67, the o becomes a g, "meet at nogn", and nobody notices. Confidentiality and integrity are two jobs. The key does one, the checksum from Errors and Redundancy does the other: computed over the sent bytes, it reports "error, send this frame again". You need both.

**What stays in the clear (Frame 31).** The class frame: preamble, type, length, payload, checksum, end marker. What gets encrypted is the payload, and only the payload. The receiver has to recognise the preamble before it can decrypt anything; encrypted, it would be a different pattern. The length controls the reading-in before the payload is complete. The checksum belongs to the frame's layer and is computed over the bytes actually sent, that is over the encrypted payload, and sent along in the clear. A listener sees that a frame is coming and how long it is; the sending time would tell them that anyway. Layers as in Abstraction and Layers: each does its own job, and encryption is a layer above the frame.

**Pack first, then lock (Frame 32).** A packer lives on redundancy, on repetitions and uneven frequencies, and good encryption removes exactly those: afterwards every byte is as likely as every other, the packer finds nothing and even adds its table. Measured on the raw parrot with a one-time key: zip first, then encrypt, 132 809 bytes; encrypt first, then zip, 196 776 bytes. The wrong order does not save 64 kilobytes, it costs 168 bytes of table. The receiver does it backwards: decrypt first, then unpack.

**Hidden in the parrot (Frame 33).** One more idea that is often confused with encryption. The parrot twice, and the one on the right holds 24 576 characters of text, a good twelve pages, without any visible difference. In every colour value the lowest bit is almost worthless, it changes the colour by one part in 256. That is where the text sits, bit by bit; 98 556 colour values are changed by exactly 1.

**The lowest bit, made visible (Frame 34).** Show only the lowest bit of the red channel, white for 1, and the original looks like noise with traces of the picture. With the text inside, stripes appear: ASCII letters all start with a 0 and resemble each other, and the pattern gives itself away at once. Whoever knows the trick reads the message out in a blink, without any secret. The capacity is the arithmetic from Analog and Digital: 256 by 256 points times three colour values are 196 608 values, one bit per value, divided by eight bits per character: 24 576 characters.

**Hidden is not secret (Frame 35).** The two ideas are opposites. Encryption hides nothing: everyone sees that a message is going, and it even looks suspicious, like noise. Steganography encrypts nothing: the text is in the picture in plain form, only where nobody looks. You can have both, in this order: encrypt first, then hide. Then whoever searches finds only noise in the lowest bit, and noise was there before.

## To close (Frames 36 and 37)

Five sentences to take away: light is public, the key protects the content. XOR with the key, twice, and the message is back. The method may be known, the key may not, and it has to be long. The key needs a second path, a table or a padlock. A key prevents reading, not changing: keep the checksum, pack first, then encrypt. And the postcard from the start: readable by everyone, understood by one. For Challenge 4: whoever is done early encrypts the payload, writes the method and key length into the specification, not the key, and lets another team read along.
