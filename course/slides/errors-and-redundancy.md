# Lecture notes: "one bit went wrong" (Errors and Redundancy)

These lecture notes explain the deck on the concept Errors and Redundancy for reading afterwards. They follow the order of the slides (the numbers are frames, build-up steps count individually) and can be read as a text of their own. For Challenge 4 they are required reading: there the hash decides whether a run counts.

## Part 1: it will go wrong (Frames 3 to 7)

**The scratched sticker (Frame 4).** You scan these codes every day: at the parcel locker, on the e-scooter, on the menu, on the slip with the wifi password. Some of these stickers have been hanging outside for two years. A corner is torn off, a scratch runs across, dirt and rain did the rest, and still the scan works on the first try. Nobody repaired the sticker, nobody asked again. Why does that work? Three questions for this deck hang on it: why does something inevitably break on the way? How do you notice before the damage becomes visible? And what does noticing cost?

**A piece is missing, and it still reads (Frame 5).** The same code without the weather, but with a hole. The QR code on this slide points at `docs.lifi-project.de`, and a piece has been knocked out of its middle: 172 of its 1,089 squares are gone, close to a sixth of the surface. It is still readable, and that is calculated, not a magic trick. A decoder reads it in the original, blurred, rotated by seven and by twelve degrees, shrunk to 260 pixels, as a low-quality JPEG and even out of the exported slide image, even when that is scaled down to 640 pixels wide. The reason lies in the way the code is built. It carries 100 bytes. 36 of them carry the data, that is the 29 characters of the address plus padding. The other 64 carry nothing that is not already there; they exist only for the damage. So almost two thirds of this code is dead weight as long as nothing happens, and whoever designed it paid that price in advance, without knowing whether a scratch would ever come. That is redundancy, and with it the question of this deck is on the table: how much dead weight is worth it?

**Errors are arithmetic, not bad luck (Frame 6).** Take a good link on which one bit in a thousand goes wrong, so 99.9 percent arrive correctly. Ten characters, that is 80 bits, then arrive completely clean with a probability of 92.3 percent; that feels good. Do the same a hundred times, and the probability that all hundred runs were clean is 0.03 percent. And for two kilobytes in one go, the 16,384 bits of the payload in the final, it is 0.0000076 percent. That is not bad hardware, that is multiplication: every single bit has to make it, and the probabilities multiply. That is why the question is not how to prevent errors, but how to notice them.

**The check as a box, twice (Frame 7).** The same way of thinking as with compression and encryption, the IPO model. A fixed rule turns many bytes into a few bits. At the receiver, the bytes and the bits go together into a second box, and what comes out is not the message but an answer: it fits, or it does not. These extra bits carry nothing that is not already in the data; they are pure redundancy, and that is exactly why errors show up with them. Note the contrast with compression: there we threw redundancy out, here we put it back in on purpose. That is not a contradiction, but a decision about what you spend your bits on.

## Part 2: how you notice (Frames 8 to 19)

**One bit that only says even or odd (Frames 9 to 11).** You know the check bit as a chain of gates from the toolbox; now it gets a purpose. Sender and receiver agree on even parity: the ninth bit is chosen so that the number of ones is even. The byte `1011 0010` has four ones, so the check bit is 0. If one bit flips on the way, there are three, an odd number, and the receiver knows: this byte is broken. It does not know which bit it was, only that something is wrong. That is enough if you can ask again.

**Where one bit is not enough (Frame 12).** If two bits flip in the same byte, the number of ones is even again, and parity reports nothing. It catches every odd number of errors and misses every even one. That is not a design flaw, but the honest price of a single extra bit, and it is the general rule of every check: it is never a proof, but a filter with a known mesh size.

**From one bit to a checksum (Frame 13).** Instead of one bit per byte, one number over the whole frame, computed from all the bytes by a fixed rule; the field for it is already in the class standard. A damaged frame then has only a chance of one in 256 of producing exactly the expected checksum by accident. With 16 bits it is one in 65,536, with 32 bits one in 4,294,967,296, and that is exactly what PNG and ZIP carry along as CRC-32. Two things to remember: the checksum never says where the error was, and it repairs nothing. More check bits do not catch more errors either; they let fewer slip through.

**A fingerprint for a whole file (Frame 14).** Now the third level. A hash is a fingerprint of a file: a fixed rule takes all the bytes and squeezes them into one single number, written in hexadecimal notation. The decisive thing is the length. A single letter gives `ca978112ca1bbdcafac231b39a23dc4da786eff8147c4e72b9807785afee48bb`, the sentence "meet at noon" gives `50a8d1d0…`, and the whole parrot photo with 196,662 bytes gives `c96f9a5a…`: all three exactly 64 characters. A hash is always the same length, no matter how big the file is. That is why two files can be compared without having both: it is enough to compare the fingerprints, and those are 32 bytes long instead of two kilobytes. The method here is called SHA-256, and it is the same one your computer uses to check whether a download is intact. About the name: "hash" literally means chopped meat, and that fits. The bytes are chopped up and mixed until nothing can be recognised any more; that is also why you cannot compute backwards.

**And one bit changes everything (Frame 15).** This is the property that matters. The fingerprint of the raw parrot is `c96f9a5adeeda470…`, the fingerprint of the same parrot with a single flipped bit is `c793da57f7f8bf7f…`. Exactly one of 1,573,296 bits changed, and the two fingerprints have nothing to do with each other any more: seven of 64 characters match, and that is roughly as many as would match between two completely unrelated files, because with sixteen possible characters per position you hit four on average. There is no similarity, no partial match, no slightly-broken. That is why a hash is a test and not a measure: it says same or not same, and it says nothing more.

**Three checks, three jobs (Frame 16).** So that nobody mixes them up. The parity bit covers a byte and reports an odd number of flipped bits. The checksum covers a frame and says: this frame is broken, send it again; that is the basis for targeted retransmission. The hash covers the whole file and says: these two kilobytes are exactly those two kilobytes. In the final, the hash decides. Important: a run in which every frame passed its checksum can still fail at the hash, for instance if a frame went missing entirely or arrived twice. That is why you need both.

**One flipped bit in the raw picture (Frame 17).** Now on the file itself. One bit flipped in the middle of the picture data of the raw parrot: the file opens, the parrot is there, and nobody sees anything. Only the zoom to sixteen by sixteen points shows it. The colour value `(240, 45, 38)` has become `(240, 45, 166)`, one red has become a slightly different red, one colour value out of 196,608. That is exactly why the hash counts in the final, and not whether the picture looks pretty. "It displays" proves nothing at all.

**The same bit in the packed picture (Frame 18).** The same picture, the same kind of error, a different format. In a PNG no point stands on its own any more: packing works by pointing back at what was written earlier, that was the trick from the compression deck. One wrong bit, and the unpacker loses the thread; the file cannot be opened at all any more, the viewer only reports "unrecognized data stream contents". PNG notices it by itself as well, because it carries a CRC-32 for every block: stored `a831c3c1`, computed after the flip `cb5ebce6`. The lesson is uncomfortable and important for the final: the better you pack, the more fragile the transmission becomes. Compression and checking belong together.

**The demonstrator (Frame 19).** Inside a File: change a byte in the picture data, and the picture obeys; change a byte in the header, the width for instance, and the viewer refuses or shows nonsense. The question that goes with it: which of the two errors is worse, and which one is noticed sooner?

## Part 3: what it costs (Frames 20 to 22)

**Redundancy is paid in time (Frame 21).** The two kilobytes of the final at ten bits per second take 27.3 minutes if nothing is added. A 32-bit checksum per 256-byte frame costs 1.6 percent, so 27.7 minutes, and in exchange you know of every frame whether it is intact. A parity bit per byte costs 12.5 percent, 30.7 minutes, and still does not catch everything. And if you send every bit three times so that you can repair without asking back, you send three times as long: 81.9 minutes instead of 27.3. These four numbers are the whole decision from Part 4, just in minutes.

**The demonstrator (Frame 22).** Where Did the Bits Go?, now with a different eye: push the error rate up and watch how the retransmissions eat up the time while the picture does not get any bigger.

## Part 4: notice or repair (Frames 23 to 26)

**Repair without asking (Frame 24).** It works without asking back: send every bit three times, and the receiver takes the value that occurs at least twice. `1 1 1` turns into `1 0 1` on the way, the majority says 1, and the receiver repairs it on its own. That is why it is done where asking is not possible: you cannot ask a space probe to repeat the frame. The price is brutal: three times the sending time, always, even when nothing breaks. And if two bits of the same triple flip, the receiver repairs in the wrong direction. The sticker from the beginning belongs to the same family, only with more elegant arithmetic: a QR code on a lamppost cannot ask anyone for a repeat, so it pays in advance, with 64 protection bytes on 36 data bytes, and in exchange it can take a hole.

**Repair by asking (Frame 25).** The return channel is allowed in the final, and this is the reason. A checksum per frame, and if one does not fit, exactly that one frame is sent again. As long as nothing breaks, this costs 1.6 percent; if something breaks, it costs one frame. Two things belong in your specification for this: a frame number, so that the sender knows which frame is meant, and a waiting time after which it sends again on its own, in case the reply gets lost.

**Which one do you build? (Frame 26).** Whoever can ask back checks and asks; whoever cannot ask back has to pay in advance. You can ask back. So build in the checksum, not the repetition code, and put the time you save into speed. And check your check: flip a byte on purpose and see whether your receiver really reports it. A checksum that never fires looks exactly like one that works from the outside.

## Conclusion (Frames 27 and 28)

Five sentences to take away: errors are arithmetic, not bad luck; enough bits, and one goes wrong. Redundancy is bits that carry nothing new and in exchange rule something out. Parity catches odd errors, a checksum almost everything, the hash decides. A picture that displays proves nothing. And: noticing costs 1.6 percent, sending everything three times costs 200 percent, asking again only costs something when something really breaks. The workshop assignment: a checksum in the frame, a frame number with it, the return channel for targeted retransmission, and once, on purpose, flip a byte to see whether your own check reports it.
