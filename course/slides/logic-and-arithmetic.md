# Lecture notes: what does a computer actually do? (Logic and Arithmetic)

Deck 12, the input on the concept "Logic and Arithmetic". These notes explain the slides for reading afterwards. They follow the order of the slides and give the slide numbers as the HTML deck counts them (every build-up step has its own number), but they can be read as a text on their own.

## Part 1: switches

**A cabinet full of relays (slide 4).** What can you build out of switches? A relay is a switch that is flipped by a current: when current flows through the coil, the coil pulls a contact, and a second circuit closes. That is all it can do. Two relays in a row only conduct when both are pulled; that is an AND. Two side by side conduct when one of them is pulled; that is an OR. In 1941 Konrad Zuse's Z3 computed with about two thousand of these relays, clattering away, and it could add, multiply and take square roots. The chip in your laptop does nothing different, only its switches number a few billion and they are silent. This input walks the whole way from the switch to the keypress that makes a photo brighter.

**Three things a computer does with bits (slides 5 to 7).** Storing is something you have done all semester: the file sits on the disk, and the bytes stay as they are. Moving too: the bits arrive as they left, otherwise something went wrong. The third one is new: from bits, new bits appear by fixed rules, bits that were nowhere before. Only changing them makes the computer more than a fast messenger.

**The toolbox: AND, OR, XOR (slide 8).** Three building blocks, each one a switch with a fixed rule: two bits in, one out. AND gives 1 when both inputs are 1. OR gives 1 when at least one is 1. XOR, the exclusive or, gives 1 when exactly one input is 1; so it asks "different?". Next to each building block is its truth table, the complete list of all cases. You know the shape from deck 03: input, rule, output, the IPO model at its smallest.

**And one that flips: NOT (slide 9).** The fourth building block has only one input. NOT flips the bit: 0 becomes 1, 1 becomes 0. Two rows in the table, done. A computer needs nothing more. Really, nothing.

**With a NOT behind them: NAND, NOR, XNOR (slide 10).** Put a NOT behind AND, OR or XOR, and you get three more building blocks with names of their own. Their tables are the ones from before, only flipped. The small circle at the output is the shorthand for the NOT: compute first, then flip. Why these three get names of their own is shown on the next slide.

**One gate is enough: NAND (slide 11).** From NAND alone, all the others appear. NOT, by feeding the same bit into both inputs. AND, by flipping a NAND once more. OR, by flipping both inputs first. NAND is also what a pair of transistors does on its own; the chip in your laptop consists, in practice, of NAND gates, and everything else is assembled from them. NOR, which is OR with NOT, can do the same, and we need that in a moment.

**No gate understands anything (slide 12).** Before we build, this sentence: no gate knows whether it is processing a number, a letter or a pixel. Meaning exists only for us. It is the same insight as with the type field in Challenge 4: bits mean nothing until someone says how to read them.

## Part 2: storing

**Two NOR gates, wired into each other (slides 14 to 17).** We have not yet delivered on the first verb from slide 5, storing. It comes out of the same gates. Two NOR gates, and the output of one runs into an input of the other; on the left two lines come in, set and reset, on the right q comes out. This circuit is called a flip-flop. At the start set and reset are 0, and q is 0 (slide 14). Put set to 1, and the lower NOR gives 0, the upper one gets two zeros and gives 1: q is 1 (slide 15). Take set away again, and q still stays 1, because the 1 from above holds the lower NOR at 0 through the feedback, and the 0 from below holds the upper one at 1 (slide 16). The circuit remembers the short pulse; that is one bit of memory made of two gates. reset = 1 tips the whole thing back, q becomes 0 and stays 0 (slide 17). Set, hold, clear: a memory cell needs nothing more.

**Try it: the flip-flop (slide 18).** The demonstrator "Gate Lab" on the slide shows exactly this circuit. Click set and then off again: q stays. Click reset: q flips. In the full Gate Lab in the browser there are also the stations d latch and register, which turn the flip-flop into a memory cell with a data line and then into a whole byte.

**From one bit to memory (slide 19).** Eight flip-flops side by side are a register, one byte; on the slide it holds 65, the A from deck 08, until someone overwrites it. Millions of them, each row with an address, are the working memory: address lines select a row, data lines read it or write it. As long as current flows, the feedback keeps every bit alive; power off, and the loop collapses. That is why RAM forgets when you switch off, and that is why the hard disk stores things differently, magnetically. The point: storing and processing are the same switches, only wired differently.

**Excursion: what 32-bit and 64-bit mean (slides 20 to 22).** The term turns up all the time, when you download a program or in the system settings. Above all, it means the number of address lines, so how many rows the memory may have at most. Every line is 0 or 1, and every additional line doubles the number of possible addresses. With 16 lines there are 65,536 addresses, 64 kilobytes: a home computer from 1982 (slide 20). With 32 lines there are a little over four billion, 4 gigabytes (slide 21). That is why a 32-bit Windows could never use more than 4 GB of working memory; a fifth gigabyte simply has no address. With 64 lines there are 18 quintillion addresses, 16 exabytes, more than all the RAM ever built (slide 22). That is why today we only talk about 64-bit. The number also stands for the width of the registers and of the adder that comes next; but memory is the reason you feel in everyday life.

## Part 3: adding

**1 + 1 in binary (slides 24 to 26).** What is 1 plus 1 in the binary system? The whole addition table for two bits has four rows: 0 + 0 = 0, 0 + 1 = 1, 1 + 0 = 1, and 1 + 1 = 10 (slide 24). Write all results with two digits, with a leading zero where needed, and every result has a sum digit and a carry digit (slide 25). As a table with the columns a, b, sum and carry (slide 26), this is the question that matters: which gates deliver these two columns?

**The half adder: two gates (slides 27 and 28).** The sum column reads 0 1 1 0: it is 1 when the bits are different. That is exactly the XOR column from the toolbox (slide 27). The carry column reads 0 0 0 1: only with two ones. That is AND (slide 28). Two gates, and the machine adds two bits. The thing is called a half adder, "half" because it cannot yet accept a carry from the right.

**Eight in a row: a byte adder (slide 29).** A full adder also takes the carry from the right, three bits in instead of two; that costs a few gates more, about five. Eight full adders in a row add two bytes, exactly the way you added digit by digit at school: from right to left, with carry. On the slide the example is 178 + 40 = 218, in binary 1011 0010 + 0010 1000 = 1101 1010. Remember this sum, it comes back.

**The rest of arithmetic is built the same way (slide 30).** Subtracting is adding the negative, and you get the negative of a binary number by flipping every bit and then adding one; the trick is called two's complement, and you do not need to know the name. Multiplying is repeated adding, and times two is a shift by one position to the left, just as times ten appends a zero in the decimal system. Comparing is subtracting and looking at the sign. Everything ends up at the adder, and the adder ends up at two gates. Charles Petzold sums it up in one sentence in his book "Code": When you come right down to it, addition is just about the only thing that computers do.

## Part 4: a photo, pixel by pixel

**A photo is a grid of bytes (slides 32 and 33).** You know this from the Photo Digitiser: a greyscale picture is a grid of numbers, each number one byte, 0 black, 255 white. On the slide there is a section of eight by eight pixels from the parrot, at the edge of the beak (slide 32), then the same 64 pixels with their values; the pixel marked in yellow at the bottom has the value 178 (slide 33). The numbers are the picture. The computer sees nothing else.

**Brighter: add 40 to every pixel (slide 34).** The yellow pixel, 178, becomes 218, and underneath is the same binary sum as in the byte adder, with the carry in the right place. The "brighter" filter is nothing but this addition.

**And the other 63, then all 16,384 (slide 35).** The same for the next pixel, and the next, for all 64 in the section and for all 16,384 of the whole picture; on the slide you see the section before and after, visibly brighter. No step knows anything about the picture. The same addition, very often.

**More filters, same toolbox (slide 36).** First, the mistake every beginner makes once: 230 + 40 = 270, and that does not fit into eight bits. An adder that throws away the ninth carry turns almost white into almost black, and a bright sky gets black speckles. The repair is a comparison (is the result greater than 255?) and a decision (then 255), which is called clamping; the demonstrator on the next slide always does this. Then the filters themselves. Darker: subtract 40. Negative: 255 minus the value, and bit by bit that is a NOT, because 255 in binary is 1111 1111. Black and white: compare with a threshold; if the value is at least 128, it becomes 255, otherwise 0; the same decision you made with your colours in Challenge 1. Blending two photos: add and halve, and halving is a shift by one position to the right. Every filter you know from a photo app is of this kind, only with more arithmetic; blurring, for instance, computes with nine pixels at once.

**Try it: the pixel filter (slide 37).** The demonstrator lets you get your hands on the whole thing, with the colour photo of the parrot, so with three bytes per pixel. First "step": one pixel, its bytes, the binary addition with carries, the result, and the counter jumps to 1. Then "run": the whole picture, the counter runs up to 16,384 pixels and tallies the gate circuits, a few hundred thousand. For bright pixels the calculation panel shows how the comparison holds the 255 before storing. The number at the bottom is the point: tens of thousands of additions, each made of a few dozen gates, in the blink of an eye. The computer is not clever. It is fast and stubborn.

## Part 5: one gate, two jobs

**XOR over a byte: a check bit (slide 39).** XOR over all eight bits of a byte, link by link: each gate takes the result so far and the next bit. At the end one bit says only one thing: an even or an odd number of ones. 1011 0010 has four ones, so the check bit is 0. The sender attaches this one bit. If a single bit flips on the way, the number of ones is odd, and the check bit no longer matches. That is the bridge to the next input: checksums are processing in the service of transmission.

**XOR with a key: encryption (slides 40 and 41).** The same gate, a different purpose. XOR with a key flips exactly the bits where the key has a 1; the result looks random (slide 40). XOR once more with the same key flips them back, and the message is there again (slide 41). Encrypting and decrypting are the same operation with the same key. And a key of all zeros flips nothing; then you are sending plain text and do not notice.

**A processor (slide 42).** The photo shows a processor like the one in every laptop. Everything that came up in this input happens inside it, only billions of times over and silently. Looking back: switches, adders, arithmetic, filters, check bit, encryption, all from the same box. What makes a computer powerful is not the cleverness of its building blocks, but their number and their speed. Once you have understood that, you will never again be surprised that a computer "understands" nothing and can still do everything you write down for it, step by step.
