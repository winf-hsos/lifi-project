# Lecture notes: overview (Overview)

These notes explain the deck giving an overview of the module and the project, for reading afterwards. They follow the order of the slides (the numbers are frames, and build-up steps count separately) and can be read as a text of their own. The deck is shown on the first day and brought up again and again during the semester, then with whatever is currently on the agenda highlighted. The page that goes with it is "Overview" on the website.

## Part 1: the goal (Frames 3 to 7)

**A photo, two laptops (Frame 4).** There is a photo on one laptop. How does it get to the other one if the only things you are allowed to use are a small lamp and a light sensor? No cable, no Wi-Fi, no stick. That is the semester in one sentence: at the end, this photo crosses a gap of a hand's width, from one computer to the other. And right away the second sentence, because it is the more important one: this module is not about sending photos with light. Nobody needs that. The lamp is a trick.

**The device (Frame 5).** A small box with two things on its front: a lamp and an eye. The lamp is an RGB LED, three little lamps in one, and you tell each of them how brightly to shine. The eye is a colour sensor that reports four numbers: red, green, blue and light overall. A thin wall between them stops the device from seeing its own lamp. Two such boxes face each other on the table. Because the one opposite is turned around, its eye looks at my lamp and mine at its lamp; so every device can send and receive. The way back is there from the start, but it is only used in the last challenge.

**Why a lamp (Frame 6).** Everything interesting in a computer is invisible. It happens in chips, in millionths of a second, behind software that somebody else wrote. You can use a computer for a lifetime without ever seeing what it really does with a photo. The device brings a small piece of that into view: it stands in front of you and blinks. You can hold your hand in between and watch what goes wrong. What happens millions of times per second inside the computer happens here a few times per second, and you built it yourselves. Everything this module wants to teach can be seen on this table.

**The one question (Frame 7).** How can we solve complex problems with computers? That is the question above everything else, and you will find it again on every concept page. Every week is a piece of the answer.

## Part 2: one question, four questions (Frames 8 to 17)

**The map (Frames 9 to 13).** It builds up in five steps. First the big question on its own. Then four arrows to four questions: How do computers represent information? How do they store it? How do they transfer it? How do they process it? Under each question stands what the computer does with bits along the way, as a coloured box: making bits (orange), storing bits (green), sending bits (magenta), processing bits (blue). Below that the concepts, eighteen of them, each belonging to exactly one question and carrying its colour. The colour stays the same for the whole semester: where a slide is orange, it is about representing; where it is magenta, about transferring. Last of all, right at the top and in grey, five ways of working that you need everywhere and not just for one of the four questions: cutting problems, the model of input, processing and output, algorithms and programs, measuring and experimenting, abstraction and layers. On the first day nobody has to understand any of these concepts. You are only meant to see that they exist and where they belong.

**The project on the map (Frame 14).** Why this project, of all projects? Because the device touches all four questions. The photo sits as a file on the disk: storing. Its bytes represent a picture: representing. A checksum computes over them so that the receiver knows whether everything arrived intact: processing. And the light carries them across: transferring. That is why the device is the means and not the end.

**The four questions, one sentence each (Frame 15).** Representing: to the computer the photo is a list of numbers, and the numbers are states of something physical, a voltage, a magnetised spot, a flash of light. Storing: the photo has to sit somewhere before it is sent, and somewhere after it has arrived. Transferring: the receiver does not know when a letter begins, and the light it sees is never exactly what was sent. Processing: somebody has to turn the numbers back into a picture and check whether they arrived correctly.

**What the exam is about (Frame 16).** The concepts. The project is the material on which you explain them, but it does not replace understanding them. Whoever passes every challenge and still cannot say why digital transmission tolerates noise does not pass the exam. That belongs in the first hour, not in the last.

**The map on the website (Frame 17).** Every concept has a page: a summary with guiding questions, the explanation, the slides from the session, practice questions in the exam format, further reading. The page "Overview" is the way in. It carries the same map and says for every concept in which challenge you need it.

## Part 3: five challenges (Frames 18 to 26)

**Only ever one new difficulty (Frame 19).** Five challenges: The Spark (can you bring the device to life?), The Alphabet (how many signals can your receiver tell apart?), The Word (can you send a whole word, and how fast?), The Listener (does the receiver find the start of a message on its own?), The Packet (can you send a real file, correct and fast?). Challenge 0 is not a competition; it ends when the LED lights up because you told it to, and the sensor opposite notices. From Challenge 1 on, all teams compete under the same conditions, and each challenge builds on the result of the one before: the alphabet from 1 sends the words in 2. The timing from 2 is what the listener in 3 has to find again without help. And 4 puts everything together and opens up the way back. Because only one thing is ever new, when something breaks you usually know where to look.

**Where each challenge sits on the map (Frames 20 to 24).** The same map, one challenge highlighted per step and the rest dimmed. Challenge 0: the ways of working Input, Processing, Output, Algorithms and Programs, Cutting Problems. Challenge 1: Analog and Digital, Symbols and Information, Signal and Noise, and measuring properly for the first time. Challenge 2: Code Systems, Number Systems, Sampling and Synchronization, the first agreement, and the beginning of the layers. Challenge 3: Protocols, in full. Challenge 4: Logic and Arithmetic, Errors and Redundancy, Memory and Storage, Compression, Throughput and Limits, and, if you like, Encryption. You can see the order in the colours: first orange and magenta, representing and transferring; green and blue, storing and processing, come all together at the end, when a whole file travels through the air for the first time.

**What every challenge produces (Frame 25).** Three things, worth keeping from the first day on. Measurement logs: what did you change, what did you keep the same, what came out? Your partner still understands a good log a week later. A description of your solution, detailed enough that another team could rebuild it; from Challenge 3 on it becomes a complete specification of your protocol. And the log of mistakes: two or three places where the assistant suggested something and a measurement proved it wrong. That is not a confession. It is the most useful document you write, because it shows where the assistant's knowledge ends and yours begins.

**What counts (Frame 26).** The ranking in the competition does not count towards your grade. Passing all challenges is what admits you to the exam. The exam is about the concepts, one person at a time, in conversation.

## Part 4: how we work (Frames 27 to 30)

**Two people, two devices, one link (Frame 28).** The project is built for two people. Each of you gets a device, so together you own both ends of the link. If you only build the sender, you have to guess what the receiver sees; you do not have to guess, you can look. The two ends have different jobs; split the work and swap now and then, so that both of you have seen both sides. You program in Python, in VS Code, with a small module that talks to the device, and with an AI assistant from the first line on.

**The lamp has the last word (Frame 29).** Your assistant knows nothing about your device. It will tell you that eight colours make a good alphabet. Is that true? There is one honest way to find out, and that is an experiment. Measure before, measure after, and write down what the assistant got wrong. AI is allowed and welcome: for programming, for explaining what you did not understand in the lecture hall, for looking up your components. The interesting part is where it stops. How far your link reaches and how fast it goes, it can only guess. You can measure.

**The demonstrators (Frame 30).** For almost every concept there is a small toy in the browser: switch a byte, break a file, watch a transmission with noise, encrypt a text. They are embedded in the concept pages and all stand side by side on an overview page, with the concept as their label.

## Closing (Frames 31 and 32)

Five sentences to take away: one question, four questions, representing, storing, transferring, processing. Eighteen concepts, five colours, one map that keeps coming back. Five challenges, each with exactly one new difficulty. The exam is about the concepts, and the project is the place where you meet them. And: the lamp has the last word. The next step: install the software, read the hardware page once so that the parts have names when you plug them in, and start Challenge 0. By the end of the semester a picture crosses the gap. If you then say that was a module about light, I have done something wrong.
