# Lecture notes: where does a message start? (Protocols)

Input for session 9, deck 11. These lecture notes explain the slides for reading afterwards; they follow the order of the slides, but can be read as a text of their own. The slide references count frames, that is every build-up step individually, exactly like the counter in the deck. This deck is built differently from most: the plenary experiment "The Listener" sits in its middle, because you are meant to invent the most important idea of the session yourselves before it gets a name.

## Part 1: who says "now"?

**Mayday (Slide 4).** A distress call on marine radio always begins with "mayday" three times. Why three times? Because the channel is noisy and nobody knows when a call begins. A single word gets lost or could be chance; three identical words in a row are a pattern that noise does not produce. And the call has a fixed shape: the call three times, then who is calling whom, then the message, then "over" (I am done, your turn) or "out" (conversation finished). Sailors did not invent this out of a love of order, but because without a shape nothing gets through. From now on, your receiver faces exactly this problem.

**So far, a human said "now" (Slide 5).** In Challenges 1 and 2 somebody started both programs shortly one after the other, pressing enter on both machines. That made the start clear, and the receiver knew the length of the message as well. This help now disappears: the human pressing enter was your frame, and you now have to replace it.

**The receiver hears the room (Slide 6).** The receiver runs before the sender starts. It sees readings all the time, because the room is never dark: on the slide, the grey, irregular blocks on the left and on the right. Somewhere in this stream is the message, the coloured blocks in the middle. How is the receiver supposed to know where it begins, and how where it stops? Nothing about the numbers themselves tells it. The two red question marks are the programme of this session. Remember this stream, it comes back later, symbol by symbol.

**The sentence of the session (Slide 7).** A bit stream without a frame means nothing. Bits only become a message once both sides know where it starts, where it ends and what it means. No sensor delivers this frame; you agree on it.

**What a running receiver needs (Slides 8 to 10).** Three questions, and your agreement has to answer each of them: where does it start? Where does it end? And what does the receiver do when something goes wrong, for instance when an expected pattern never arrives? The third question is the easiest to forget, because in the normal case it never shows. An agreement that only covers the normal case leaves the receiver guessing in the failure case, and then every implementation behaves differently. The session does not settle the first two questions with slides but with you; the third gets its own slide in Part 3.

## Part 2: the listener

**Round one (Slide 12).** At the front, at the visualizer, the lecturer first sends a few random colours, then a message of about five colours, then random colours again. Nothing is announced. The room writes along and is supposed to name the message afterwards. It has no chance, and that is exactly the point: you have just experienced what your receiver experiences without a frame. The slide has two lines into which what the room wrote down is entered.

**Round two (Slide 13).** The same game three times, but every message begins with the same conspicuous sequence, say red-blue alternating three times. Again, nothing is announced. By the second or third pass the room spots the start by itself. By what? By the sequence that was never there before and now comes every time. With that, the room has invented something without the word ever being said.

**You just invented the preamble (Slide 14).** Only now the name. A preamble is an agreed, conspicuous pattern in front of the message; the slide shows the same stream as on Slide 6, only with the highlighted pattern in front. It carries no information, it only says "from here on". It replaces the human who used to press enter. Two properties make it usable: it is agreed, and it does not occur in noise. That is why it consists of two active colours and not of light and dark: an LED that is off looks to the sensor exactly like the room, so a pattern with an off-part would be half noise. With that, the question mark at the start is gone; the one at the end remains, and Part 3 deals with it.

**One red is not a start (Slide 15).** Why does the pattern have to be longer than one symbol? At the top of the slide, a receiver whose start pattern is a single red: in room light a reddish value comes up by itself all the time, a lamp, a shadow, a fluctuation, and at every one of these places the receiver reports "message begins". Three such false starts are marked. At the bottom the same noise, but the pattern is the sequence red, blue, red, blue, red, blue: chance essentially never produces it, and the receiver finds the start exactly once. The longer and the more regular the pattern, the more rarely chance imitates it. If you are fighting false starts, change the agreement, not the gain or the integration time; those lift the noise exactly as much as the signal.

**What the frame costs (Slides 16 and 17).** The price of the run-up, this time worked out. The frame on the slide consists of six symbols of preamble and three symbols of end marker, so nine symbols that carry no payload. With five characters at eight bits each and two colours, the data is 40 symbols long; so 49 are sent, and nine of them carry nothing. That is about 18 percent. The second step puts the same calculation for 20 characters next to it: nine out of 169, so about 5 percent. The frame always costs the same nine symbols per message, no matter how long the message is, and that is why short messages pay the most for it. For Challenge 4 this means: few long messages rather than many short ones.

**When the data looks like the start (Slide 18).** A problem your specification has to answer: what happens if the preamble turns up by chance in the middle of a message as well? On the slide the sequence red-blue-red-blue-red-blue appears a second time, this time as part of the data. A receiver that keeps scanning for the pattern takes that for a new start, throws away what it had so far, and begins again in the middle. The problem is real and has a name in practice. There is more than one good answer, and your specification has to settle on one of them: only scan for the pattern in the state "waiting", use a length field and count instead of scanning, or reserve a colour so that the sequence cannot occur in the data at all. The first one comes up four slides later.

## Part 3: the end

**Round three (Slide 20).** Two messages directly back to back, each with a preamble, no pause. Where does the first one stop? From the room come two kinds of answers: "You say beforehand how long it is" and "You send a sign at the end". Both are right, and both are collected. This collection is the raw material for the standardisation session in two weeks.

**Two answers, two price tags (Slides 21 and 22).** The length field stands at the start and says how many characters follow, for instance "12 characters"; the receiver counts along and afterwards knows that it is over. Price: the field has to arrive without errors, otherwise it ruins the whole message, and its size limits the message, with 8 bits to 255 characters. The end marker is an agreed symbol after the last character, a "stop"; the receiver stops as soon as it arrives, and the message may be any length. Price: the same question as with the preamble, the marker must not occur by chance in the data, otherwise the message ends too early. The clean way around that is a **reserved symbol**: a colour that does not occur in the data alphabet. That costs capacity, because your alphabet gets smaller. Neither of the two ways is the right one. You pick one and write it down.

**The same reading means different things (Slides 23 to 27).** Your receiver needs a memory, and on these five frames the same stream from Slide 6 runs through symbol by symbol. At the top the two states "waiting" and "reading", at the bottom the stream with a read head. As long as it is waiting, everything that is not the pattern is noise and is discarded. Once the pattern is complete, the state jumps to "reading"; from then on every symbol is a data symbol and is kept. The end marker brings it back to "waiting". Watch the colours: the same red is noise in one state and a data symbol in the other. The decision is not made by the reading but by the state. And the arrow back is the most important one in the picture: without it, the second message never arrives at the acceptance test.

**A state is only a variable (Slides 28 to 30).** In Python this is twelve lines: a variable `state`, an endless loop, and one branch per state. In the state "waiting" the only check is whether the start pattern is complete; in the state "reading" every symbol is appended until the end marker arrives. The last build-up step shows in yellow the two lines most people forget: after the output, the state has to go back to "waiting" and the message has to be emptied. If you forget this, you have a receiver that can handle exactly one message; that is the most common fault at the acceptance test, and the live change "two messages back to back" finds it. You have to write the function `preamble_complete` yourselves. It remembers how much of the pattern has already been seen, so it is a small memory again.

**When the end never comes (Slide 31).** Now the third question from Slide 10. Halfway through the message somebody puts a hand into the beam, the rest never arrives, and the end marker does not come. The obvious thought is: too bad, this message is lost. That is not true, and the slide shows why. The receiver is in "reading" and no longer scans for the preamble at all, because it only does that in the state "waiting". The next message begins properly with its pattern, and the receiver skips it completely. So it does not lose this message; it loses every message from now on. What is needed is an abort rule: if the end marker has not arrived after an agreed time or number of symbols, it discards what it started and goes back to "waiting". And if you do output fragments, then labelled: half a message that passes as a whole one is worse than none at all.

## Part 4: the agreement

**A protocol is an agreement that somebody else can implement (Slide 33).** Nothing about it is self-evident, and all of it has to be settled before the first bit: the symbols, the start, the end, the timing, the failure case. The test is not whether it works between the two of you. The test is whether somebody who does not know you can build a receiver from it. That is exactly what happens next week.

**The test for your specification (Slide 34).** In your head, cross out everything you know but did not write down. Is the rest enough? The slide shows a specification that looks complete: symbols, start, character code, end. Two lines are missing, and both are red. First, how long a symbol stands: to you that is obvious, but for another team it makes the specification unbuildable, because without the symbol duration they cannot even sample the start pattern reliably. Second, what happens when the end never comes. A complete example, say `HI` as the full sequence of symbols including start and end, saves more specifications than any explanatory text. The template `protocol-template.md` asks for both.

**What happens next (Slides 35 to 37).** This week every team builds its own frame and writes it down. Next week you swap specifications and build another team's receiver; that is the only honest test of your documentation, and failing there is a finding about the document, not about the partner team. After that the class agrees on a common frame, built from your proposals: whose preamble was more robust, who had thought of a length field?

**To finish (Slide 38).** This is how standards really come about: not on a drawing board, but from comparing solutions that work. The shipping container is the example: only when everybody agreed on the same dimensions did it turn into world trade, and not because those dimensions were the best, but because they were the same. A standard is an agreement between people, not a technical necessity. What lies below the frame, colours, speed, calibration, stays yours afterwards as well.
