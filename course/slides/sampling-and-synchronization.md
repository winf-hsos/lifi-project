# Lecture notes: when is it your turn? (Sampling and Synchronization)

Input for session 7, deck 09. These notes explain the slides for reading afterwards; they follow the order of the slides, but they can be read as a text on their own. The slide references count frames, so every build-up step separately, exactly like the counter in the deck.

## Part 1: why it collapses

**The orchestra (slide 4).** Eighty people produce one shared signal, and every one of them can keep time. And yet, without the person holding the baton, everything would be out of step within a minute, and not out of incompetence: small differences in tempo add up, and nobody notices their own error. Your two devices have exactly this problem, and in this session it gets a name.

**Your homework (slide 5).** You raised the symbol rate until the transmission tipped over, and the tipping rates are in the table. Two things stand out: every team finds a limit, but not the same one, and the error patterns differ. That is because there are two different causes behind the tipping, and we are about to separate them.

**What a dot means (slide 6).** Before the pictures start, the reading instructions: a dot on the following slides is shorthand. In reality the receiver measures many times within one symbol and averages; that is exactly what your `read_stable()` from session 5 does. The one dot stands for this averaged value and sits where the middle of the readings lies. Without this slide, all the pictures that follow would be a small lie.

**Cause one: no two clocks run alike (slides 7 and 8).** On top are the sender's time slots, underneath the receiver's dots. Both have agreed on the same rate, and still the receiver measures each symbol a tiny bit later than planned. Slide 7 shows the first symbols: every dot lies in the middle of its slot, all fine. On slide 8 the same dots keep moving to the right, until the last ones (red) sit in the wrong slot. The error per symbol is harmless, its sum is not. And because the error grows with the elapsed time while the slot shrinks with the rate, the problem hangs on speed: at one symbol per second it goes unnoticed for minutes, at ten per second you are off within seconds.

**Cause two: the sensor needs time to look (slide 9).** The sensor does not deliver an instantaneous value; it collects light over its measurement window and reports the average. That is the integration time, 50 ms on the slide. On the left the symbols are 100 ms long, so longer than the window: the measurement falls entirely inside one colour, and the reported value matches one of your profiles. On the right the symbols are 40 ms long, so shorter than the window: every measurement contains a colour change, the sensor reports a mixture of both colours, and that matches nothing. The pale dots in the window are the individual readings from slide 6. On the left all of them lie in the same colour field, on the right two of them lie in the neighbouring field, and exactly these two ruin the average. The nasty part: there is no error and no warning, just a meaningless number. Shortening the window helps against the mixing but increases the scatter; that is the same trade-off as in session 5.

**The real question (slide 10).** Both causes are versions of the same question: how does the receiver know when a new symbol begins? The sender knows, it does the switching itself. The receiver only sees light. There are two fundamentally different answers. Either time tells it, or change tells it. Both come with a price tag, and parts 2 and 3 read out the labels.

## Part 2: the clocked way

**When does it tip? (slides 12 and 13).** This is how you have worked since Challenge 1: start on both computers at the same time, use the same rate, then each side counts for itself. The drawing plots the accumulated clock error over time, for a difference of half a per cent: a straight line that simply keeps growing. The dashed yellow line is the threshold, half a symbol, so 50 ms at ten symbols per second. Slide 13 marks the point where they cross: after ten seconds. From then on the receiver measures in the neighbouring symbol. Go ahead and check it, it is one line: 0.5 % of 10 seconds is 50 ms. The threshold is what matters. It is not a whole symbol of error that tips the transmission, but half of one, because the dot sits in the middle of its slot and has only half the width of room on either side. And the same arithmetic explains why slow is so much more robust: at one symbol per second half a slot would be 500 ms wide, and the same clocks would last a hundred seconds.

**Eighty people, one clap (slide 14).** We try this out in the room. On top in the drawing: a shared start, after that everyone claps on their own, once a second. Every clap is a cloud of dots, one dot per person, and the cloud gets wider with every second, until nobody claps together any more. Underneath, the same people, but every five seconds a pulse comes from the front (the yellow lines): the cloud only grows until the next pulse and then starts again from zero. Agreeing once is not enough, agreeing again and again is. Afterwards the room has the difference in its ears, not only on the slide.

**Recurring markers (slide 15).** The same in a transmission: at fixed intervals the sender slips in a marker, and the receiver re-anchors its time grid on it. The drawing shows the sawtooth: the error grows, jumps to zero at each marker, grows again. So it can never get larger than what accumulates within one block. And how do you build a marker? As a **reserved symbol**: a colour that does not occur in the data alphabet, or a short dark pause. All that matters is that the receiver cannot confuse it with any data symbol. And how does the receiver reset its clock with it? Not by merely seeing the marker in its grid; that would achieve nothing. It samples faster than symbols arrive and remembers **when** the marker begins: there it anchors the grid afresh, next symbol boundary equals marker start plus symbol duration. The remaining error is at most one sampling step. The orchestra does exactly the same: it registers not only that a beat came, but when. And a nice twist on the side: at exactly one point the clocked way borrows the idea of the clock-free way, reacting to a change, only once per block instead of at every symbol.

**The Drift Simulator (slide 16).** The demonstrator on the slide makes both visible. Two tasks: first, let it run and watch the display "half a slot off after" until the red rings appear. Second, set the marker interval to eight and watch what happens to the error and what the "marker overhead" says about it. You can also open it at home: <https://winf-hsos.github.io/lifi-concept-demos/drift-simulator/>.

**The price tag (slide 17).** Starting once costs not a single symbol, but it lets the error grow without limit. Markers limit the error but carry no payload: send a marker every eight symbols and you give up an eighth of your rate. Whether that pays off depends on how bad your clocks really are, and that is a question of measurement, not of belief.

## Part 3: the clock-free way

**The naive idea (slide 19).** Every colour is a symbol, and a colour change means "next one, please". The receiver no longer needs a clock; it simply measures as often as it can and reacts only to changes. But two identical symbols in a row produce no change: sent red, red, green, seen one long red and a green. The idea is good, it just is not finished yet.

**The fix: the transition carries the bit (slide 20).** It is not the colour that carries the information, but the change to the next one. From every colour two successors are allowed, one stands for 0, one for 1; the colour itself is forbidden as its own successor. That way the problem case can no longer occur, because there is always a change. In the triangle of the three colours, the clockwise step means 1, the anticlockwise step 0; the sequence red, green, red, blue gives the bits 1 0 0. The receiver measures as often as it likes and decodes only on change.

**The price in bits (slide 21).** Synchronization freedom is paid for with capacity: as states, k colours carry log2(k) bits, as transitions only log2(k−1), because the colour itself drops out. With three colours that is 1 bit instead of 1.58, with four 1.58 instead of 2. And here a trap hides for everyone who thinks in powers of two: for a clean 2 bits per transition you need five colours, not four. Whoever encodes over transitions wants an alphabet of size 2 to the power n plus 1.

**Clock-free does not mean time-free (slide 22).** The most common wrong conclusion: "We got rid of the clock, so time is no longer an issue." Two decisions about time remain. During a change the sensor measures mixed values, so there has to be a threshold for how long a colour must hold steady before it counts as a new state; otherwise the receiver invents symbols out of the intermediate values. And the number of transitions per second the link can manage still decides the throughput, with exactly the same limits as in part 1.

**What the file costs in minutes (slides 23 and 24).** Whatever the strategy, in the end this calculation counts: t = n / (r · b), with n the bits to send, r the symbols per second and b the bits per symbol. Slide 23 shows the formula and its three quantities, slide 24 works through an example. 16,000 bits are 2 KB, the payload from Challenge 4. Clocked, four colours carry 2 bits per symbol; times five symbols per second that is 10 bit/s, so about 27 minutes. Clock-free, the same four colours carry only 1.58 bits per transition, so about 34 minutes. That is the price of synchronization freedom, in minutes. The calculation has exactly two levers, bits per symbol and symbols per second, and everything in this session decides about both. How far these levers can be turned at all is the subject of `throughput-and-limits`.

**The workshop task (slide 25).** First the clapping experiment with the whole room, then in the workshop: determine your own maximum rate systematically, with evidence, not with gut feeling, and record it in your profile. Also write down a reasoned preliminary decision: clocked or clock-free? It is not binding, but it forces you to take the price tags seriously. Which strategy a team chooses remains its own decision in Challenge 3.

**To finish (slide 26).** No two clocks run alike. That is not a quirk of your laptops but a property of the world. The only decision left to you is what you do about it.
