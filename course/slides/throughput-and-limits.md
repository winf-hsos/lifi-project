# Lecture notes: why is it still not done? (Throughput and Limits)

Input for session 13, deck 14, the first of the two decks in this session. These lecture notes explain the slides for reading afterwards; they follow the order of the slides, but can be read as a text of their own. The slide references count frames, that is every build-up step individually, exactly like the counter in the deck. The third of the three levers, compression, has had its own deck since 13 September 2026 (`slides/compression/`, "say it shorter"); here it only gets its place and its price.

## Part 1: where the time goes

**Two kilobytes, twenty-seven minutes (Frame 4).** That is not an invented number, it is yours. Two kilobytes are 16,000 bits; with four colours and five symbols per second that is 10 bits in a second, so a good 26 minutes. The calculation was already on a slide at the end of session 7, but you only understand it once you are sitting next to it, waiting. And that calculation does not yet contain a single symbol of frame or a single repeat.

**The screen everyone knows (Frames 5 and 6).** A game or an update, a progress bar, a rate, a time remaining. Two different things stand there. The rate is a measurement: that many bytes arrived in the last second. The time remaining is a calculation made from it: what is still missing, divided by that rate. In the second step the rate drops from 12.4 to 4.1 MB/s, and the time remaining jumps from three to nine minutes, although almost nothing has happened. Exactly these two numbers are the subject of this deck, only for the light link: what is the rate, what does it depend on, and what does it say about the time a file needs. Except that on your screen it does not say megabytes per second, but bits.

**Gross and net (Frame 7).** At the top, what the link would carry if every symbol were payload. At the bottom, what is left after synchronisation markers, preamble, end marker and repeats. What matters is how this comes about: not a single bit was lost, everything arrived correctly; more than half of it was just administration. Anyone talking about throughput always has to say whether they mean gross or net.

**Where the 24 went (Frames 8 to 11).** The calculation step by step. 24 bit/s if everything were payload. Every fourth symbol is a marker, so three quarters remain: 18. Of what is left, 40 per cent goes to preamble and end marker, because many short messages are being sent: 10.8 remain. The last build-up step shows the most common mistake in red: 25 plus 40 per cent is **not** 65 per cent lost. The second deduction only applies to what the first one left over. Shares that take effect one after another are multiplied, not added.

**Pulling the wrong lever (Frame 12).** The obvious thought is: send twice as fast, get twice the throughput. On the left it works, a symbol lasts 100 ms and the measurement window is 50 ms, and the measurement falls cleanly inside it. On the right the rate is doubled, and now a symbol is exactly as long as the window; the window visibly reaches across a symbol boundary. On paper 40 bit/s, in reality nothing readable arrives any more. Pulling a lever that is not the bottleneck gains nothing; pulling it past the bottleneck makes things worse. The order is: first shorten the window, then raise the rate, then check whether the scatter still allows it.

**Your limit is a measurement (Frame 13).** Three things it is not. Not the sensor's datasheet: that describes the component and knows neither your distance nor your ambient light nor your optics. Not the best team's number: that holds for their setup. And not the arithmetic rate times bits per symbol: that says what comes out for values you choose, not which values your link supports. There is only one way to find the limit: raise the rate step by step, count the errors, record the last error-free setting.

**What your link really does (Frame 14).** The measured net rates are collected in the session; the table is pre-filled and editable. The interesting column is not the number but the third one: how was it measured? An estimated value is not a measured value, and whoever measured with a cardboard tube around the sensor has measured a different link from everyone else.

## Part 2: three levers

**Three levers, and only three (Frames 16 to 19).** The same formula as in session 7, now read from the other side: `t = n / (r · b)`. Three quantities, so three levers, and each one has a different price. More bits per symbol, that is a larger alphabet: costs safety margin, because the bands get narrower. More symbols per second: costs measurement window, and that is where most teams find their limit. Fewer bits in total: costs a few milliseconds of computing time, nothing else; how to do that is the subject of the second deck. What is **not** on this list is a faster computer. The bottleneck is the link; at ten symbols per second every laptop spends most of its time waiting.

**The same 20 bit/s, twice (Frame 20).** Team A sends 10 symbols per second with four colours, Team B 5 symbols with sixteen colours. Both come to 20 bit/s, because for throughput only the product counts. But as soon as something breaks, it breaks for different reasons: Team A is running against the measurement window, Team B against the scatter. So when you compare yourselves with another team, compare not only the number but also what it hangs on.

**The third lever has its own deck (Frame 21).** Fewer bits in total means compression, and on your light link it pays off far more than when copying a file to a USB stick. The reason is that the link is the bottleneck: you have computing time in abundance, transmission time you do not; a few milliseconds of computing save minutes of sending. And because the method works before the link, it is the only lever that has the same effect on every setup, whether your bottleneck is the measurement window or the scatter. How it works and where it stops is explained in the deck "say it shorter".

**Three levers, one file (Frames 22 to 25).** 16,000 bits at five symbols per second and four colours: 27 minutes. Eight colours instead of four give 3 bits per symbol instead of 2, so a factor of 1.5: 18 minutes. Compressed to 60 per cent: 16 minutes, so slightly better, and at a much lower risk, because compression only costs computing time, while the larger alphabet costs safety margin. Both together: 11 minutes. The levers act independently of one another, which is why their effects multiply. The 60 per cent is a typical value for text; where it comes from is shown in the second deck.

## Part 3: what it costs to be sure

**Every tenth one again (Frame 27).** With the return channel the receiver acknowledges, and whatever arrives damaged is repeated. Every tenth message is hit. Nothing is lost in the process, everything arrives. But for ten delivered messages you send eleven, and that leaves 91 per cent, not 90. The difference is small here because the error rate is small. Work it out once with every second message: three transmissions for two deliveries, so two thirds. As soon as a link gets really bad, the curve gets steep.

**Acknowledge each, or a block (Frame 28).** Every acknowledgement is a small transmission of its own, and in between the link stands still because both sides are waiting; at ten to fifty bits per second that is no small item. Acknowledging per block saves these gaps, but then a single error costs the whole block. Which way wins depends on your error rate, and you measured that in Part 1. This is not right or wrong, but a decision with a number underneath it.

**Throughput is not delay (Frame 29).** The difference that is confused most often, outside this course as well. Throughput says how much arrives in a running stream; delay says how long the first character takes. A fat line can react sluggishly, a thin one can be quick. In your case, the eight payload symbols of a one-byte command come with another nine symbols of frame, the recognition of the preamble and the receiver's reaction time. A single short command is therefore slower by a large factor than the rate suggests.

**The workshop assignment (Frame 30).** First, measure your own net rate and write down how it was measured. Second, find the lever that is cheapest to pull on your own setup, with the number that proves it; the answer is different for every team, and that is exactly the point. Third, set a block size for the acknowledgements and justify it, with the measured error rate, not with a feeling.

**To finish (Frame 31).** The hourglass from the beginning, now as an answer. The narrow point decides how much gets through per second, and no program in the world changes that. What gets through is up to you: markers, frames, repeats, and in the second deck the question of whether it has to be that many bits at all.
